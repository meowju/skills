# Quick Scripts — Extended Reference

> *These scripts are maps for when you don't know what to say — not performance scripts, but guides for honest self-leadership.*

## Research Basis

These scripts are grounded in:
- **Attachment theory** (Bowlby): The anxiety that surges post-breakup is attachment-system activation, not love. Naming it as withdrawal changes the frame entirely.
- **Rumination research** (Nolen-Hoeksema): The longer you ruminate without acting, the harder it gets. But "acting" means concrete behavior change, not sending one more text.
- **Self-compassion research** (Neff): Self-kindness in grief is the single biggest predictor of recovery speed — more than "understanding what went wrong."
- **Timeline research**: Acute grief peaks around 2-4 weeks for most people. What feels like it will last forever typically eases noticeably by week 6-8.

## The Scripts

### When they say "I can't stop thinking about them"

> That's because your brain is still running attachment chemistry. That's not love — that's withdrawal. The thoughts will quiet down when you stop feeding them. The way out is through, not through more rumination.

**Why this works:** Names the mechanism (attachment chemistry) rather than pathologizing the person. Separates the feeling from the identity ("I must still love them"). Gives agency — withdrawal eases when you stop reinforcing it.

**Follow-up if they push back:**
> The fact that you can't stop thinking about them doesn't mean you should be with them. It means your nervous system is calibrated to expect them. That calibration changes. It takes time, not more contact.

---

### When they say "I wasn't good enough"

> You were with someone who couldn't show up fully. That's not a measurement of your worth — it's a measurement of their capacity. And capacity is not fixed. People can grow. But you're not responsible for waiting around while they figure it out.

**Why this works:** Separates self-worth from relationship outcome. Reframes the partner's limitations as the relevant variable, not a reflection of the griever's value.

**Common follow-up — "But they said I was the problem":**
> People who leave often rewrite the story so they feel less guilty. "It was you" is sometimes easier for them to say than "I wasn't ready" or "I didn't know how." Look at behavior, not narrative. What did they actually do, not what are they saying now?

---

### When they say "I want to text them"

> Write the text. Save it to Notes. Wait 24 hours. Then read it back as if a friend showed you that text from a stranger. Would you tell that friend to send it?

**Why this works:** Uses external perspective-taking (rumination research: self-distancing reduces emotional flooding). The 24-hour window lets adrenaline dissipate. Most "emergency texts" look very different 24 hours later.

**If they say "But what if they move on?"**
> They might. That is survivable. It feels like an extinction event right now because grief magnifies probability. But people remarry after divorces. People find partners after years alone. The fear of them moving on is about your timeline, not theirs. Your healing is not on their schedule.

---

### When they say "I feel like I'll never find someone"

> You just lost something that took up a lot of space. After any demolition, the space looks terrifying. But space is not emptiness — it's potential. The right person doesn't complete you. They fit alongside you. That happens differently than you think.

**Why this works:** Validates the grief without arguing with the catastrophizing. Uses the demolition metaphor to reframe the disorientation of singledom. Introduces the "fit alongside" model — which counters the Hollywood completion myth.

**Evidence-based anchor:**
> Relationships after breakup are not competitions. Your ex getting married doesn't mean you lost. It means two paths diverged. Statistically, most people who wanted a committed relationship and worked on themselves eventually found one — even after long marriages that ended.

---

### When they say "My ex is married / pregnant / seemingly ahead of me"

> You're comparing your chapter 3 to their chapter 10. That's not a measurement — it's an optical illusion. Their life progressing does not mean you failed. It means the path diverged. Close the tab. Feel the grief. Then find one thing today that is yours and move toward it.

**Why this works:** Directly names the comparison trap mechanism. "Close the tab" is behaviorally concrete. "Find one thing that is yours" redirects without invalidating.

**If they saw it on social media:**
> Social media is a highlight reel of other people's best moments, filtered through what gets engagement. No one posts "feeling lonely at dinner again" or "wondering if this was right." You're seeing the product shot, not the product. Unfollow or mute — not out of spite, but because your nervous system needs a rest from the feed.

---

### When they say "I keep looking at their social media"

> Tracking an ex's online activity is a modern grief behavior with ancient neurological wiring. Your striatum doesn't know the difference between a former partner and an available one — it just knows reward prediction. Every scroll that shows something painful triggers a mini-rejection response. This is not weakness. This is chemistry. But chemistry responds to distance, not engagement.

**The intervention:**
> Delete the app that shows you their content. Remove the ability to check without friction. This is not avoidance — it is creating the conditions for your nervous system to recalibrate. You cannot out-think a dopamine circuit. You can only stop giving it data.

**If they say "I need to know if they're doing better":
> "Better" according to what metric? Their new partner's Instagram? The fact that someone appears happy online tells you nothing about the quality of their life. And even if they genuinely are doing better — that also tells you nothing about whether staying would have made *you* better. Other people's trajectories are not your reference point.

---

### When they say "I just want to know why they left"

> "Why" is a reasonable question. But closure is not a delivery — it's a decision. Sometimes the reason is real ("they weren't ready"). Sometimes it's post-hoc rationalization ("they just wasn't feeling it"). Neither answer reliably settles the question. What you're really looking for is a story that lets you stop revisiting it. You can give that to yourself.

**The grief anniversary script:**
> Every year on the date of the breakup, your nervous system does a dress rehearsal of the original loss. This is normal. Name it: "It's the anniversary of something hard. I'm allowed to feel it." Don't push through it or white-knuckle it. Let it surface, let it pass, note what you notice.

---

### When they say "Should I reach out?"

**The 72-hour rule:**
> If you want to reach out, write what you'd send. Save it. Wait 72 hours. If you still want to send it after 72 hours, show it to a friend first. Almost never does the 72-hour version get sent. The urge to break no-contact is usually anxiety masquerading as love.

**If they genuinely need to communicate (practical coordination, co-parenting):**
> Keep it logistics-only. "I need the documents from your apartment." Not "I miss you." Not "How are you?" Logistics. Boundaries on communication are not cruelty — they are what makes functional co-parenting or shared-item exchanges possible.

---

### When someone asks "Are you okay?" (and you're not)

> "I'm working through something. Thanks for checking in." — This is honest without being a burden. You don't owe anyone a performance of okay-ness. And "working through something" is a true statement that closes the door without inviting advice.

**If they push:**
> "I appreciate you asking. I'll let you know when I want to talk about it." — This is firm without being cold. Gives you control of the timeline for disclosure.

---

### When friends ask "Have you moved on?"

> "I'm not tracking it that way anymore." — This signals progress without performing recovery. It means you've stopped measuring yourself against a timeline.

---

### When someone says "There are plenty of fish in the sea"

> "I know. Right now I'm not thinking about the sea. I'm just processing what's in front of me." — Deflects toxic positivity without shutting down the friend's attempt to help.

---

### When your ex badmouths you to mutual friends

> The best response to someone rewriting history is to stay congruent with your own behavior. Don't correct the narrative publicly — people who matter will notice the discrepancy. People who don't notice weren't worth the argument anyway.

**If it reaches you through the mutual friend:**
> "I understand if that story is complicated for you to hear. I'm not going to relitigate this. I know what happened in this relationship, and I know how I showed up. That's what I'm working with."

---

### When you want to "accidentally" run into them

> Your limbic system is not trying to reunite — it's trying to complete the unfinished pattern. Seeing them will not complete it. It will restart the withdrawal clock. The only way through is to let the pattern stay unfinished. That's not romantic. It's also not tragic. It's just neuroscience.

## The Litmus Test for Any Impulse

Before any reactive communication, ask:
1. **Am I acting from growth or from fear?** (Fear = trying to reduce pain. Growth = aligned with who you want to be.)
2. **Would I say this to a stranger on a train?** If no, don't send it to an ex.
3. **Am I trying to change their narrative or mine?** If you're trying to change theirs, don't.
4. **Is this for them or for me?** If it's for you (to feel better, to get closure, to be seen), write it and don't send it.
