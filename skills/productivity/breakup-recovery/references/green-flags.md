# Green Flags in Relationships — Full Reference

> *This is the extended reference for the condensed green flags section in SKILL.md. The skill body contains the summary and litmus test. This reference holds the full content for deeper study.*

## What Green Flags Actually Signal

Green flags are not about finding a perfect person. They are about recognizing when a relationship has a functioning foundation — one that can withstand normal stress without collapsing.

The distinction that matters: **green flags don't prove someone is perfect, they prove the relationship has a foundation capable of withstanding normal stress.** You don't need someone who makes you feel perfect all the time. You need someone who can be honest, accountable, and curious about you when it's hard — not just when it's easy.

## The Core Green Flags (Expanded)

### 1. They can disagree without destroying you

Contempt (sneering, eye-rolling, name-calling, stonewalling) is the single most reliable predictor of relationship failure across decades of Gottman's research. Conflict without contempt means both people can hold their ground and still respect each other.

The test: can you raise a genuine concern and have it addressed without the conversation turning into an attack on you? Not "did they immediately agree" — that's not the goal. The goal is: can you be in conflict without being diminished?

### 2. They apologize specifically

"I'm sorry I did X" — not "I'm sorry you feel that way." Specific accountability is a learned skill. Blanket deflection ("I'm sorry you took it that way") is a pattern, not a mistake.

A specific apology names the behavior, acknowledges the impact, and doesn't come with conditions. "I'm sorry I cancelled our plans last minute without checking if you had other things going on. That was disrespectful of your time" is a real apology. "I'm sorry if my actions upset you" is not.

### 3. They respect your "no" without punishment

Boundary-setting is the test of whether a relationship is built on genuine mutuality or on one person's accommodation of the other's needs. If boundary-setting consistently produces guilt, silent treatment, or disproportionate retaliation, the foundation is broken — regardless of how good the good times feel.

The signal: do they get slightly defensive in a way that passes quickly, or do they consistently make you feel like your "no" is an imposition that requires justification?

### 4. They're curious about your inner experience

They ask what you think, how you feel, what you need — and they integrate the answers, not just file them away for later use. Curiosity means: they want to understand you, not just have you be a certain way.

The test: do they remember what you've told them and reference it later? Not as leverage, but as evidence that they're paying attention.

### 5. Their actions and words match — consistently

Over time, consistency is the only real signal. Grand gestures impress; daily reliability tells you who someone actually is.

The reliability test: when they say they will do something, do they do it? When they say they'll change a behavior, does it change? Not for a week — over months. Words are easy. Consistent behavior is the only currency that matters.

### 6. They support your autonomy and your other relationships

Healthy partners want you to have a full life — not a life centered on them. If friends and family are treated as competition for their attention, that is not love. It is enclosure.

The signal: do they encourage you to see friends, pursue interests, have time apart — or do they create reasons why those things are difficult, inconvenient, or threatening?

### 7. They take responsibility in conflict

When something goes wrong, they look inward first rather than reflexively blaming you or the circumstances. This is accountability — not performance of apology, but genuine first-response of "let me understand my part in this."

The test: when you bring up something that hurt you, do they typically respond with "let me think about that" or do they immediately redirect to your role in it?

### 8. You feel safer, not smaller, over time

The relationship expands your sense of self, not contracts it. You become more of who you are, not less. Your ambition is supported, not managed. Your boundaries are respected, not negotiated.

The core question: after being with this person, do you feel more capable and more yourself — or less? Not "do they make you happy" (happiness is temporary and context-dependent), but "do they make you *more*."

## What Green Flags Are NOT

These are commonly mistaken for green flags but are not reliable indicators of a healthy relationship:

- **Chemistry** — can be intense with someone deeply unhealthy
- **Comfort in familiarity** — can be familiarity with a pattern, not safety
- **Dramatic expressiveness** ("we have such passion") — can be volatility disguised as connection
- **Shared interests or lifestyle alignment** — can coexist with fundamental incompatibility in values
- **A partner who is "good on paper" but leaves you anxious after they leave the room** — anxiety is data

## The Litmus Test

The litmus test for the green flags above:

Can you disagree with them, express a need that inconveniences them, or say "no" to something they want — without the relationship going dark?

Every healthy relationship has survived at least one real conflict where both people held their ground and neither felt diminished. If that hasn't happened yet, the foundation is still being tested. The absence of a real conflict is not evidence of health — it may mean the relationship hasn't yet been challenged in a way that reveals how it handles stress.
