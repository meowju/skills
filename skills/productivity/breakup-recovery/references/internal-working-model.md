# The Internal Working Model — Full Reference

> *"Your internal working model is not a description of reality — it's a prediction built from experience. It can be updated."*

## The Two-Component Model

Bowlby's **internal working model** has two components that operate below conscious awareness:

**Model of self:** *Am I worth loving? Worth staying for? Worth fighting for?* This determines whether you speak up in conflict, whether you believe your needs are legitimate, whether you can be vulnerable without shame.

**Model of others:** *Will people be there when I need them? Will they come back? Will they hurt me?* This determines how you interpret silence, whether closeness feels safe, how quickly you give up on connection.

These two models interact: if your model of self says *I'm not worth staying for*, your model of others says *people leave*. They're not independent — they're two lenses on the same core wound.

## Three Simultaneous Recovery Tracks

Research (Siegel, Porges, van der Kolk, and the broader trauma field) converges on three simultaneous tracks:

**1. Emotional (top-down)**
- **Method:** Therapy, journaling, meaning-making, narrative reconstruction
- **What it addresses:** The story you tell yourself about what happened — why they left, what it means about you, whether it was avoidable
- **What it can't do:** Complete the body's threat response; regulate the nervous system directly

**2. Somatic (bottom-up)**
- **Method:** Breathwork, bilateral tapping, cold-water immersion, yoga, movement, EMDR
- **What it addresses:** The body stores injury differently than the mind. The nervous system holds the threat response long after the conscious mind has "understood" it
- **Why it matters:** Trauma is stored as incomplete defensive responses (Porges). The body needs to complete its action impulse — fight, flight, or freeze — to close the threat cycle

**3. Relational**
- **Method:** Secure friends, therapist, support group, witnessing relationships
- **What it addresses:** The attachment system heals through witnessed relationship — being seen, being heard, being accompanied through the hard thing
- **Why it matters:** You cannot heal a relational wound in isolation. The wound was made in relationship; it heals in relationship

**If you've done a lot of therapy and journaling but still feel stuck:** the missing piece is usually somatic. The body needs to complete its threat response — not through understanding, but through physical completion.

## The Window of Tolerance

**Polyvagal theory** (Porges, 2001) describes the **window of tolerance** — the zone where you can feel difficult emotions without flipping into panic or numbness:

- **Hyperarousal** (above the window): panic, anxiety, racing thoughts, inability to rest
- **Hypoarousal** (below the window): numbness, flatness, dissociation, emotional shutdown

Breakup grief narrows your window. Small triggers activate large responses because your nervous system is already maximally activated from the loss.

**What widens the window:**
- **Coherent breathing:** 4 seconds in, 6 seconds out. Activates parasympathetic. Works during acute spikes
- **5-4-3-2-1 grounding** (for hyperarousal): Name 5 things you see, 4 you hear, 3 you touch, 2 you smell, 1 you taste
- **Bilateral tapping** (for stuck rumination): Alternate tapping left-right-left-right on thighs or arms; mimics REM processing
- **Gentle movement** (for hypoarousal): Walking, stretching, shaking — anything that re-engages the body without intensity

## The "Haunted vs. Hooked" Diagnostic

When the urge to reach out hits, check which system is driving it — different problems need different solutions:

**Haunted** (grief-adjacent, memory-based):
- *What it feels like:* Sadness, longing, replaying good memories, missing who they were
- *What's driving it:* The normal grief process; your brain processing the loss
- *What to do:* Process through writing, feel the feeling, let it move through

**Hooked** (addiction-adjacent, reward-based):
- *What it feels like:* Physical pull, obsessive thinking, bargaining, "just one more message"
- *What's driving it:* The same dopamine circuits that drive addiction; the attachment system is activated but can't find resolution
- *What to do:* Apply no-contact immediately. Don't negotiate with neurochemistry.

The diagnostic question: *Is this grief or is this craving?* Grief moves in waves and eventually calms. Craving relentlessly escalates. Grief eventually lets you rest. Craving wakes you up at 2am.

## Grief Anniversaries — Predictable Reactivations

Breakup grief anniversaries are predictable because the nervous system runs calendar-patterns:

- **The breakup date:** The obvious one; the nervous system marks it as the day the threat occurred
- **The first date:** The day the relationship became official — another attachment milestone
- **The day they left:** If they moved out or the separation happened on a specific day
- **Anniversaries of shared rituals:** The first trip, the first "I love you," the first fight you overcame

**What to do:** Mark them on a calendar in advance. When the day arrives, tell yourself: *my nervous system is completing an anniversary pattern. It will pass.* This is not a regression — it is a completion. The grief wave comes because the nervous system is trying to finish something it started.

**If anniversaries hit harder than expected:** This is normal. It doesn't mean you're not healing. It means the nervous system is doing its work. Let it come, let it pass, don't interpret the wave as evidence that you've gone backward.

## Updating the Internal Working Model

The model can be updated. It's not fixed. Long-term studies (Berkeley Adult Development Study; Waters et al.) show adults change attachment classification through corrective emotional experiences: sustained therapeutic relationship, a secure partnership, or secure peer connections.

The update doesn't erase the old map — it adds to it. You don't overwrite *people leave when you need them* with *people never leave*. You add *but sometimes they don't*. The old pattern is still there; the new one becomes available alongside it.

**The mechanism of update:** Repeated corrective experience. Not one epiphany — multiple small corrections over time that accumulate into a new pattern. The breakup itself, survived without being destroyed, is one of the most powerful corrective experiences available.
