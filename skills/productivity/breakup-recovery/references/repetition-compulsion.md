# Repetition Compulsion — Why You Keep Choosing the Same Type

> *This is the extended reference for the "Why Do I Keep Choosing Them?" section in SKILL.md.*

## What Is Repetition Compulsion?

Repetition compulsion is the unconscious tendency to repeat distressing patterns from early life — especially in relationships. Coined by Sigmund Freud in 1914 ("Remembering, Repeating and Working-Through"), it describes the mind's drive to reenact unresolved emotional experiences rather than consciously remember them.

In the context of romantic relationships, this means: you are not randomly attracted to unavailable, dismissive, or emotionally harmful partners. There is a pattern. And the pattern has a source.

**The core mechanism:** Your brain's internal working model (IWM) — the mental map of "how relationships work" formed in early childhood — treats familiar dysfunction as safer than unfamiliar health. An anxious-avoidant dance feels like love. A stable, consistent partner feels boring or "not right." The wound recognizes itself in others.

## The Neuroscience of Choosing the Wrong Partner

Research on partner selection consistently shows people gravitate toward others whose attachment style mirrors their caregivers':

- **Anxious attachers** tend to choose avoidant or emotionally unavailable partners — the push-pull dynamic mirrors their childhood attachment experience and feels "familiar"
- **Avoidant attachers** often choose anxious partners, or choose partners who can handle their emotional distance
- **Disorganized attachers** frequently cycle between both patterns

This is not destiny — it is a habit. And habits can be updated.

**The dopaminergic loop:** Choosing the unavailable partner triggers the same mesolimbic dopamine reward circuits as addiction. The intermittent reinforcement cycle (hot-cold, push-pull, breadcrumbing) creates stronger neural imprinting than consistent reward. Your brain literally becomes habituated to uncertainty — and interprets it as attraction.

**The cortisol spike:** The anxiety of an unpredictable partner raises cortisol. In the short term, elevated cortisol + dopamine creates a neurochemical sensation that gets misread as "chemistry" or "passion." It is not passion — it is a stress response.

## The Three Manifestations in Breakup Recovery

### 1. Choosing the Same Type Across Relationships

The most common expression: serial relationships with the same dysfunctional pattern. Each partner has a different name but the emotional dynamics are identical. This is the "different body, same wound" pattern.

**Diagnostic question:** When you list your past partners, do the relationships feel like variations of the same story? If yes — repetition compulsion is operating.

**What it is NOT:** Choosing people who share a *superficial* trait (e.g., all blondes) is not repetition compulsion. It's repetition compulsion when the *emotional pattern* repeats: same power imbalances, same communication failures, same cycles of pursuit and withdrawal.

### 2. Returning to the Ex

The most acute version: returning to an ex despite knowing, intellectually, that the relationship was harmful. The unconscious logic: "This hurt but it was familiar. The unknown of being alone is scarier."

This is why no-contact is so important for anxious attachers specifically — the urge to return is not a sign of true love, it is a compulsion to repeat.

### 3. Self-Sabotage in Good Relationships

The most insidious version: meeting a genuinely healthy, available partner — and systematically undermining the relationship. Picking fights, creating distance, manufacturing reasons to leave.

This is repetition compulsion at its most covert. The pattern is so internalized that security itself feels threatening.

**Why this happens:** A healthy relationship activates the attachment system in a new way — it asks for vulnerability, intimacy, and trust. For someone whose early experience taught that closeness leads to pain, genuine safety can activate fear. The sabotage is a defense — "if I leave first, it won't hurt as much."

**Key insight:** Self-sabotage in a good relationship is not evidence that you don't love the person. It is evidence that your nervous system does not yet trust the relationship as safe. That is different from a genuine incompatibility.

## The Internal Working Model: Your Brain's Invisible Map

Your internal working model (IWM) is a set of unconscious beliefs about:
- Whether you are worthy of love
- Whether others are trustworthy
- Whether closeness is safe or dangerous

This map was formed in early childhood and is updated through corrective emotional experiences throughout life. Breakup recovery, done consciously, is one such corrective experience.

**The updating mechanism:** Every time you:
- Sit with the urge to reach out and *don't act* → you practice "I can survive without this"
- Grieve fully instead of suppressing → you update "pain can be processed, not just endured"
- Choose a healthy response over an habitual one → you build a new neural pathway

...you are literally revising your IWM.

## How to Break the Pattern

### Step 1: Name the Pattern Without Shame

The first intervention is recognition. Write down the emotional pattern of your past 3 relationships — not the facts, the *dynamics*. Who pursued whom? Where did the power imbalances sit? What happened when you or they pulled away?

**Pattern example:** "I always end up with someone who is emotionally unavailable when I need them most. I chase, they withdraw. I pull away, they pursue. The intensity feels like love."

Naming the pattern is not self-blame — it is gaining leverage on the unconscious.

### Step 2: Understand the Source Without Dwelling in It

Understanding that your pattern was formed in childhood is important. Dwellling in it ("my parents did this to me, therefore I am permanently shaped") is not. The goal of understanding is not to blame your parents — it is to *disidentify* with the compulsion.

A useful reframe: "This is an old habit, not a permanent sentence."

### Step 3: Practice the Opposite Behavior Consciously

If your pattern is pursuing unavailable partners, practice the discipline of *not pursuing*. When the urge to reach out arises, label it ("this is the compulsion, not the feeling"), sit with the discomfort, and redirect.

If your pattern is self-sabotage, practice *doing nothing when the urge to create distance arises*. The skill here is pausing — not never feeling the urge, but not acting on it automatically.

### Step 4: Build a New IWM Through Accumulated Corrective Experiences

This is earned security — and it takes time. Each corrective experience (a relationship where you are treated consistently well, a friendship that provides reliable emotional attunement, therapeutic work that gives you a new relationship with your own emotions) updates the internal working model.

**The earned secure finding (Waters et al., 2000):** Adults who shifted from anxious/avoidant to secure did so through "sustained corrective emotional experiences" — not through insight alone. You update the map by living new territory, not by studying the old one.

## The Crucial Distinction: Pattern vs. Person

Not every difficult relationship is repetition compulsion. Sometimes a relationship fails because of genuine incompatibility, a specific situational stress, or one person's unaddressed issues.

**The difference:**
- **Genuine incompatibility:** You can name what specifically was wrong, you feel grief without self-doubt, you can identify what you learned
- **Repetition compulsion:** The pattern repeats across relationships, you blame yourself or them in rotating ways, the grief is accompanied by a sense of stuckness or déjà vu

The goal is not to become risk-averse. It is to become *pattern-aware*.

## For Friends Supporting Someone in This Pattern

If you are supporting someone who keeps choosing the same type:
- Do not say "why do you keep doing this?" — it increases shame
- Do say: "I've noticed this pattern in your relationships. I'm not judging you — I think it might be connected to something deeper, not something broken in you."
- Avoid validating the new partner too quickly — the repetition compulsor is skilled at making unhealthy partnerships seem reasonable from the inside
- Be patient — this pattern can take years to shift, not weeks
