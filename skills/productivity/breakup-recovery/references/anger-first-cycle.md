# The First Anger Cycle — Completion and Recognition

## The Four-Stage Cycle

Anger after a breakup does not announce when it is finished. But there is a reliable pattern: the first complete anger cycle has four stages, and recognizing them prevents two common mistakes — quitting too early or staying stuck past completion.

### Stage 1: Trigger

A specific memory or situation reactivates the anger. The body braces. Heart rate rises. The trigger is usually a specific violation, betrayal, or painful moment from the relationship — or from the breakup itself.

**What this looks like:** You suddenly remember something they did, or you hear news about them, and you feel a surge of heat, tension, or agitation.

**What this is NOT:** Trigger does not mean any passing thought. The somatic charge distinguishes a trigger from ordinary rumination.

### Stage 2: Escalation

The anger builds. The story gets repeated with full emotional charge. Details resurface. The body mobilizes for action.

**What this looks like:** The initial surge builds — you feel more angry, the narrative gets elaborated, the sense of injustice intensifies. The same violation gets replayed with the full emotional weight each time.

**Critical distinction:** This is not rumination. Rumination is the cognitive loop of "why did this happen, what does this mean about me, could I have prevented it." Escalation is the nervous system's activation response — it is protest energy, not analysis.

### Stage 3: Peak and Discharge

The peak is when the body needs to move. The mobilized energy needs a physical exit. Without a physical outlet, the peak resolves incompletely — leaving residual activation that surfaces again later.

**What this looks like:** The anger reaches maximum intensity — hot, urgent, demanding. The body wants to act. Physical discharge routes: writing, hitting a pillow, intense exercise, screaming in a car, vigorous swimming, strength training.

**Discharge is not completion:** Screaming, crying, or hitting a pillow after anger is discharge — a physical outlet for mobilized energy. Discharge is step 3 of the cycle, not the whole cycle. Many people stop here and believe the anger is done.

### Stage 4: Resolution

The nervous system registers that the threat has been addressed. A quiet settling arrives. The physiological activation drops. The same trigger no longer produces the same surge.

**What this looks like:** After discharge, sitting quietly. The agitation gradually reduces. You feel tired but clear. The original violation is still in memory, but it no longer activates you the same way.

**How long does this take?** The complete cycle can take 20 minutes to several hours. The discharge-and-settle protocol (below) helps the resolution complete.

## The Two Common Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Stopping at Stage 2

Rerunning the story mentally without a physical discharge route. The escalation phase feels productive — the brain is "working through it." But escalation without discharge is rehearsal without performance. The nervous system has mobilized for action it never took.

**The result:** The anger doesn't complete. It surfaces again days or weeks later at full intensity, because the nervous system never received the signal that the threat was addressed.

### Mistake 2: Confusing Discharge with Completion

Discharge (step 3) is not the same as resolution (step 4). Screaming, crying, or hitting a pillow feels like release — and it is real release of activated energy. But completion requires the nervous system to register that the threat is resolved. Without the settling phase, discharge can feel like it worked but the anger returns.

## The Discharge-and-Settle Protocol

After physical discharge (exercise, writing, sound), sit for 5 minutes without your phone, without talking, without music. Let the nervous system register that the action is complete.

**How to sit:** In a chair, feet on the floor, hands open. Not lying down (you may fall asleep — which is fine but not resolution). Just sitting. The body needs to complete the threat-response arc: mobilization → action → resolution signal → settling.

**What to notice:** Whether the agitation has genuinely reduced. If it has not, the cycle may not have completed. Return to discharge (brief, not prolonged — you don't want to loop).

## Completion Criteria

**The one-week test:** A week after the peak, replay the original injury in your mind. If the same charge returns at full intensity, the cycle did not complete. If the charge is lower — or the memory is present without the surge — the cycle is moving through.

**The somatic marker:** Before and after the cycle, rate your body's activation level (0 = completely calm, 10 = maximum agitation). If the after-rating is lower than the peak rating by at least 3 points, the cycle worked.

**The completion signal:** A feeling of quietness that is different from numbness. Numbness is dorsal vagal — flat, disconnected. Resolution is ventral vagal — present, settled, clear. The difference matters.

## Multiple Cycles

Most people need multiple anger cycles before the original injury stops activating them. Each cycle addresses a specific violation or trigger. The number of cycles is not the measure — completion is.

The anger cycle may need to run several times because:
1. Different triggers activate different memories
2. The first few cycles may have been incomplete (stopped too early)
3. New realizations about the violations may re-activate the anger

This is not a sign of failure. It is the process working.
