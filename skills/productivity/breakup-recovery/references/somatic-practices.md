# Somatic Practices for Breakup Grief — Full Reference

> *The body does not lie. When talk therapy reaches its limit, somatic (body-based) interventions are where the nervous system completes its response.*

## Why the Body Is Not Optional in Grief Recovery

Talk therapy (top-down) works on the narrative and meaning-making layer of grief. But the nervous system stores the injury differently — in threat-response patterns, muscle tension, breathing habits, and stress chemistry. The somatic (bottom-up) track is where physical completion happens.

Research basis: Porges' Polyvagal Theory (2001); Ogden & Minton's Sensorimotor Psychotherapy (2000); van der Kolk's trauma research (*The Body Keeps the Score*, 2014); EMDR research (Shapiro, 2001).

## 1. Vagus-Nerve Breathing (Coherent Breathing)

**What it is:** Breathing at approximately 5 breaths per minute — in for 4–5 seconds, out for 5–6 seconds. The exhale is deliberately longer than the inhale.

**Why it works:** The vagus nerve is the primary channel of the parasympathetic nervous system. When you exhale slowly, vagal tone increases, heart rate variability (HRV) improves, and cortisol drops. Studies on HRV biofeedback show measurable parasympathetic activation within 5 minutes of coherent breathing practice (Lehrer et al., 2000).

**When to use it:** Before any grief-focused activity — therapy, journaling, difficult conversations. It widens the window of tolerance so that emotional processing actually lands rather than looping.

**How to do it:**
1. Sit comfortably, spine upright
2. Breathe in through your nose for 4–5 seconds
3. Breathe out through your nose or mouth for 5–6 seconds
4. The exhale should feel slightly extended — not forced
5. Continue for 5–10 minutes
6. If your mind wanders, return to the count

**Caveat:** If extended exhale feels suffocating or increases anxiety, try equal breathing (in 4, out 4) and build toward longer exhales gradually. Vagal tone varies between people.

## 2. Bilateral Tapping for Stuck Rumination

**What it is:** Alternating left-right physical stimulation (tapping knees, forearms, or using a bilateral sound device) while maintaining awareness of a difficult feeling or memory.

**Why it works:** Bilateral stimulation activates the brain's information-processing system in a way similar to what happens during REM sleep — the brain's natural mechanism for metabolizing emotional memories. This is the mechanism behind EMDR (Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing), which is an evidence-based treatment for PTSD and has been adapted for complex grief ( Schubert et al., 2011).

**When to use it:** When a memory, thought, or feeling is "stuck" — you keep returning to it, it won't resolve, talking about it doesn't help. Also useful at night when intrusive thoughts prevent sleep.

**How to do it:**
1. Sit with your feet flat on the floor
2. Alternate tapping left knee → right knee → left knee → right knee
3. As you tap, hold the difficult feeling or memory in awareness
4. Name what you're feeling: "I feel sad. I feel angry. I feel abandoned."
5. Continue for 5–10 minutes, or until the intensity reduces
6. You can also use EMDR-trained practitioners or bilateral audio tools (bilateral music, pulsing sounds)

**Note:** For clinical/complex PTSD or complicated grief disorder, seek a trained EMDR therapist. This self-directed version is for mild-to-moderate stuck grief.

## 3. Cold-Water Face Immersion (Mammalian Dive Reflex)

**What it is:** Applying cold water to the face — face washing, splash, or cold cloth on the back of the neck — for 15–30 seconds.

**Why it works:** The mammalian dive reflex (the same mechanism that allows seals and whales to dive for long periods) is activated by cold water on the face. It produces rapid parasympathetic activation: heart rate drops, breathing slows, and acute anxiety decreases within 30–60 seconds. This is one of the fastest somatic tools for interrupting a panic or anxiety spiral.

**When to use it:** Acute anxiety spikes, panic attacks, 3am catastrophic thinking spirals, moments when the nervous system is in full fight-or-flight and nothing else is working.

**How to do it:**
1. Splash cold water on your face repeatedly for 15–30 seconds
2. OR hold a cold pack or cold cloth against the back of your neck
3. Breathe steadily while doing it
4. The effect begins within the first few seconds and peaks around 15–20 seconds
5. Keep a cold pack in the fridge specifically for this purpose

**Caveat:** Not suitable for people with cardiovascular conditions without medical consultation. The dive reflex does lower heart rate — if you have heart conditions, use vagus-nerve breathing instead.

## 4. 5-4-3-2-1 Grounding (Sensory Grounding)

**What it is:** A structured sensory-awareness exercise used in trauma treatment (Ogden & Minton, 2000) to interrupt acute hyperarousal and bring the nervous system back to present-moment reality.

**When to use it:** Panic, dissociation, flooding, catastrophic rumination, feeling "unreal" or disconnected from your body.

**How to do it:**
1. **5 things you can SEE** — Look around the room and name 5 things. Be specific. "A blue mug on the desk."
2. **4 things you can physically TOUCH** — Feel the texture of your clothes, the chair under you, your feet on the floor. Name 4.
3. **3 things you can HEAR** — The hum of the refrigerator, distant traffic, your own breathing. Name 3.
4. **2 things you can SMELL** — This is the hardest one. If nothing is available, name 2 smells you remember (coffee, perfume, grass).
5. **1 thing you can TASTE** — A lingering taste in your mouth, or recall one.

The sequence activates the present-moment sensory cortex, which competes with and reduces activation in the threat-processing amygdala. Used in PTSD treatment (Hesterberg &踩) and adapted for acute grief.

## 5. High-Intensity Movement for Hypoarousal

**What it is:** Brief, structured physical exertion — brisk walking, jumping jacks, stair climbing, running — used when the nervous system is in hypoarousal (shutdown, numbness, dissociation) rather than hyperarousal.

**Why it works:** The nervous system has two threat responses: fight-or-flight (hyperarousal) and shutdown (hypoarousal). Grief can produce both. When you're flat, numb, disconnected — you're in hypoarousal. Physical exertion raises sympathetic nervous system activation, which brings arousal back into the window of tolerance without requiring emotional processing. This is bottom-up regulation.

**When to use it:** Numbness, feeling "not real," inability to feel anything, flat affect, dissociation, the sense that grief has gone "underground."

**How to do it:**
1. Brisk walk for 5–10 minutes, or
2. 10–20 jumping jacks, or
3. Run up and down stairs, or
4. Any physical activity that raises heart rate without requiring emotional engagement

**Note:** The goal is to raise arousal gently — not exhaust. If intense exercise feels like self-punishment, choose gentler movement (stretching, slow walking). The difference is in the intention: regulated movement vs. performed suffering.

## 6. Progressive Muscle Relaxation (PMR)

**What it is:** A systematic tensing and releasing of muscle groups, developed by Edmund Jacobson (1938), that produces direct parasympathetic activation through muscle discharge.

**When to use it:** Nighttime insomnia, physical tension preventing sleep, generalized anxiety that won't settle, the body feeling "tight" or "closed."

**How to do it (the standard sequence):**
1. **Feet** — Curl toes downward, hold 5 seconds, release. Then spread toes, hold 5 seconds, release.
2. **Calves** — Point toes toward face (like standing on your heels), hold, release. Then flex foot the other way, hold, release.
3. **Thighs** — Squeeze thigh muscles tight, hold, release.
4. **Abdomen** — Pull navel toward spine, hold like bracing for a hit, release.
5. **Hands** — Make a fist, hold, release. Spread fingers wide, hold, release.
6. **Arms** — Bend elbows and make muscles tight, hold, release. Then extend arms and tense triceps, hold, release.
7. **Shoulders** — Shrug toward ears, hold, release. Then pull shoulders back, hold, release.
8. **Face** — Squint eyes, hold, release. Then open mouth wide like a yawn, hold, release.

**Timing:** Hold each tense for 5 seconds. Release for 10 seconds. Breathe normally during releases. Total practice: 15–20 minutes.

**Research note:** PMR has been shown to reduce cortisol and improve sleep quality in anxiety and stress populations (Kwekkeboom & Cherwin, 2010). Particularly useful as a pre-sleep routine for the insomnia-grief cycle.

## Integrating Somatic Practices Into Your Recovery Routine

**Morning:** Vagus-nerve breathing (5 minutes) before getting out of bed — sets parasympathetic baseline for the day.

**Midday:** If you feel stuck in rumination — bilateral tapping for 5–10 minutes while naming feelings.

**Acute moments:** Cold-water face immersion for anxiety spikes. 5-4-3-2-1 grounding for dissociation or flooding.

**Evening:** Progressive muscle relaxation before bed for insomnia. If numb — high-intensity movement earlier in the day.

**Key principle:** Somatic practices are not a replacement for grief processing — they are what make grief processing possible by keeping your nervous system within the window of tolerance. If you're outside the window, the practice comes first.
