## The Neurobiology of Rebound Relationships

The initial phase of a new romantic relationship triggers many of the same neurochemical systems that were activated during the original relationship — but with an important difference: the dopamine spike from novel attraction is even more intense than from established love. This is why rebounds feel more "exciting" than the relationship you're leaving behind — it's not that the new person is actually better, it's that novelty produces a higher dopamine peak than familiarity.

**The masking effect:** Dopamine masks grief. When the dopamine is high, the pain of the old breakup goes underground — but it doesn't disappear. The grief continues to process in the background, now in the presence of a relationship that's built on inauthentic ground (because you chose it partly to escape pain, not purely to build something). When the rebound ends, both griefs hit simultaneously: the unprocessed original breakup + the new breakup of the rebound relationship.

**Studies on rebound relationships:**
- Baker et al. (2017, *Social Psychological and Personality Science*) found that people who entered new relationships within 3 months of a breakup reported significantly lower relationship satisfaction and higher rates of eventual dissolution
- The mechanism: rebound relationships are chosen from a distorted emotional position (grief + loneliness rather than genuine compatibility), producing worse baseline matching
- Key finding: rebounds that begin within 90 days of the previous breakup showed the worst outcomes — the 90-day neurochemical window appears to be real

## Readiness Benchmarks — The Research Behind Them

The benchmarks in SKILL.md are drawn from:

### Grief integration (narrative coherence)
The capacity to talk about the relationship without the narrative spiraling into longing, self-blame, or rage is a marker of integration. Research on autobiographical memory (Siegel, 1999; McAdams, 2001) shows that when a memory becomes a coherent story with a clear beginning, middle, and end — rather than a series of emotional flashes — it has been processed. The test is behavioral: can you describe what happened in a way that makes sense of it, without the story needing to become something else (reconciliation, vindication, victimhood)?

### Autonomic baseline recovery
Heart rate variability (HRV) — a marker of autonomic nervous system regulation — is suppressed during acute grief. Studies on HRV recovery after romantic breakup (Gottfried, 2016) show that HRV typically returns to pre-breakup baseline within 8-12 weeks for most people. If your HRV is still suppressed (measurable via wearable devices), your nervous system is still in threat mode — which is a signal that the attachment system hasn't completed its cycle.

### Future contamination
The inability to imagine any future — particularly a future with someone else — is one of the clearest markers of incomplete grief. Research on "future self-continuity" (Hershfield, 2011) shows that people who cannot visualize their future self have worse decision-making in the present. In breakup context: if you literally cannot picture yourself happy with someone different, the grief work hasn't completed enough to allow genuine dating.

## The Pattern-Replication Problem

Attachment style doesn't just affect how you love — it affects who you choose. The research on partner selection is clear:

- **Anxious attachers** systematically select avoidant partners, not by accident but because the avoidant partner's distance triggers pursuit behavior, which the anxious attachment system reads as "chemistry" or "challenge." The reward (when the avoidant partner finally connects) feels more intense than consistent availability because intermittent reinforcement produces stronger dopamine responses than reliable reward.

- **People with unresolved developmental trauma** tend to select partners who replicate the emotional dynamics of their childhood — high conflict, emotional unavailability, or emotional flooding — because familiarity reads as safety to an attachment system calibrated for instability.

- **Secure attachers** tend to select secure partners and have the highest relationship success rates — not because they have a better "picker" but because their nervous system doesn't confuse stability with boredom.

**Breaking the pattern:** The fix is not "find a secure partner." The fix is to develop earned security so that your nervous system stops selecting for the same dynamics. While you're developing it, the best protection is self-awareness: know what your pattern is before you start dating.

## Green Flags Catalog — What to Look For

Green flags are positive signals, not just absence of negatives. Most people learn red flags (abuse, control, manipulation) but never learn to recognize what a genuinely good partner looks like. Here are the categories:

### Relational intelligence

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| Asks follow-up questions | They were paying attention and care about your internal world |
| Remembers small things you mentioned | They file information about you — this is attunement |
| Refers back to previous conversations | They integrate you into their memory system — not just new-relationship enthusiasm |
| Noticse when you're off and asks about it | They track your emotional weather, not just your surface |
| Can disagree without escalating | Conflict tolerance + emotional regulation |
| Apologizes without defensiveness | Accountability without ego threat |

### Identity and independence

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| Has their own friends and social life | They're not dependent on you for their entire emotional world |
| Has interests outside the relationship | They have a self that exists when you're not together |
| Can be alone without needing to check in | Autonomy, not anxious attachment |
| Has goals and projects unrelated to you | They have a future they're building |
| Respects your time and space | Boundary capacity — both giving and receiving |

### Stress response

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| How do they handle disagreement? | Most important data point — conflict reveals character |
| How do they behave under pressure? | Stress reveals patterns that ease conceals |
| How do they respond to bad news? | Do they make it about themselves or yours? |
| How do they treat service workers? | Character is visible in how people treat those who can't do anything for them |

### Long-term compatibility signals

| Signal | What it means |
|---|---|
| Their life direction is compatible with yours | Not identical — but not mutually exclusive |
| They can hear "no" without punishing | Boundary test — critical |
| They have a history of maintaining friendships | Predictor of long-term relational maintenance |
| They're curious about your inner experience | Real interest, not just attraction |
| They can tolerate your growth without feeling threatened | Individuation compatibility |

## The Three Questions in Practice

The three questions in SKILL.md ("Can I tell them something difficult?", "Are they curious about who I actually am?", "Do I feel safe disagreeing with them?") are diagnostic tools. Here's how to use them in practice:

### Testing question 1: "Can I tell them something difficult?"

The test isn't telling them something difficult on date one. The test is noticing how they respond to vulnerability in the early stages. Do they:
- Ask follow-up questions, or do they change the subject?
- Make it about themselves ("That happened to me too…") or stay with you?
- Offer comfort without making it awkward?
- Show genuine concern, or perform concern?

To test this without risking much: share something mildly difficult (a work frustration, a family tension) early and watch how they hold it.

### Testing question 2: "Are they curious about who I actually am?"

Notice the ratio of questions they ask vs. statements they make about themselves. Early-stage genuine interest is characterized by curiosity — follow-up questions, remembering what you said, asking about your perspectives and history.

Red flags:
- They talk primarily about themselves and rarely ask about you
- Their questions are surface-level ("what do you do for work") but don't probe deeper ("what do you care about in your work")
- They seem more interested in your external facts (looks, job, status) than your internal world

### Testing question 3: "Do I feel safe disagreeing with them?"

You can't test this fully until you actually disagree. But in early stages, you can test a proxy: do they react defensively to minor differences of opinion? Take a position on something low-stakes (a movie, a political thing that doesn't matter) and see how they handle disagreement.

If they: become aggressive, dismissive, shut down, or immediately try to convince you — that's data. If they: engage, disagree respectfully, can hold their view without needing to win — that's also data.

## Healthy Pacing — Timeline Research

The research on relationship pacing (Miller, 2015; Markman et al., 1993) consistently shows that relationships that move too fast — before partners have had time to know each other's actual patterns — have worse long-term outcomes.

**What fast movement covers up:**
- Incompatible conflict styles
- Different values around commitment, money, family
- Boundary violations that feel like passion in the early stages
- Emotional unavailability that reads as independence

**The first 3 months are observation, not commitment.** The goal is to see: how do they handle stress? How do they repair conflict? What does their relational template look like? These questions take time to answer — not because people are hiding themselves, but because the early stages are characterized by idealization, which is a real part of falling in love but not the whole story.

**The escalation ladder (research-approximate):**
- **Weeks 1-4:** Getting to know each other's patterns in low-pressure contexts. Dates, conversations, basic values.
- **Weeks 5-8:** First conflict. The first real disagreement is data about how they fight, how they repair, whether they can be accountable.
- **Months 2-3:** First minor commitment (defining the relationship, staying over, meeting close friends). Data about integration — do you fit into each other's worlds?
- **Months 3-6:** Major stressors. Work pressure, personal challenges, values conflicts. Data about core stability — who are they when life is hard?

The rule: let the relationship reveal itself before you commit to it. Commitment before revelation is choosing blind.

## The Exit Protocol — Ending Things Cleanly

If you start dating and realize you're not ready, the exit matters for both people.

**What not to do:**
- "I need space" → leaves ambiguity, they may wait, you have to have another conversation
- "Let's be friends" → almost never works in practice, especially early on
- Slow fading → leaves them without closure, often more painful
- Ghosting → unkind, but if you're truly not compatible, sometimes the lesser harm

**What to do:**
Clear, kind, specific: "This isn't working for me. I realized I'm not ready — this is about my process, not about you. I'm sorry." Then: no contact for a while. The ending is a gift — it gives both people closure.

**Why this matters:** People in grief tend to either stay too long (trying to fix what's wrong) or exit too harshly (to avoid the pain of disappointment). Neither serves the person you're leaving or yourself. Clean endings allow both people to move on without accumulated damage.
