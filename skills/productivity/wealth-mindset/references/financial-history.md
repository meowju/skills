## Financial History: What the Big Crashes Actually Taught

Markets crash. People who understand why crashes happen make better decisions when fear takes over the room.

**1929: The Margin Loan Crash**
After the 1929 crash, buying on margin amplified losses dramatically — people who put down 10% and borrowed 90% saw their entire equity wiped out when stocks fell 90%. The lesson: leverage on volatile assets during bull markets is not investing — it is gambling with a time bomb. The crash of 1929 wasn't just bad luck; it was the predictable result of a system where too many people owned too little equity in too many assets financed with too much borrowed money.

**1987: Black Monday — The Programmed Panic**
On October 19, 1987, the Dow fell 22.6% in a single day — no fundamental news, no war, no crisis. It was a computer-driven cascade: as prices fell, portfolio insurance algorithms sold more, which caused prices to fall further, which triggered more selling. The lesson: when human traders are replaced by algorithms that all follow the same logic, markets can drop faster than anyone expects. In 2024-2025, AI-driven trading accelerated declines similarly. Your stop-loss and risk management rules must account for the possibility that exit doors disappear exactly when you need them most.

**2000: The Dot-Com Collapse**
Between March 2000 and October 2002, the Nasdaq fell 78%. Thousands of companies that raised money on "eyeballs" with no earnings went to zero. Pets.com, Webvan, WorldCom — the lesson: a company's story and a company's value are different things. Valuation multiples above 50x price in decades of perfect execution. When expectations aren't met, the fall is proportional to the altitude. Buffett's circle of competence rule exists precisely because dot-com bulls outside their circle were the ones who lost the most.

**2008: The Leverage Engine That Ate Wall Street**
The 2008 crisis killed major banks, financial institutions, and retirement accounts worldwide. The root cause: financial institutions created and sold mortgage-backed securities containing loans they knew were terrible, then bet against those same securities. The lesson: the people selling you financial products are often on the other side of the trade from you. "Due diligence" is not a formality — it is your only protection against products sold with expert confidence and zero accountability.

**2020: The Pandemic Panic and Recovery**
The fastest 30% drop in history happened in March 2020 — then the fastest recovery in history followed. By end of 2020, the S&P 500 was up 18%. The lesson: panic selling at the bottom of a non-structural crisis is one of the highest-probability wealth-destroying decisions. Always have a plan for the scenario where everything drops at once.

**2022: The Rate Shock**
In 2022, bonds fell 13% and stocks fell 20% simultaneously — the worst combined year for a traditional 60/40 portfolio since 1937. The trigger: the fastest rate hike cycle in 40 years. The lesson: when inflation returns, the traditional 60/40 portfolio can fail in ways backtesting won't show. Diversification across asset classes (real assets, commodities, TIPS) is not optional in high-inflation environments.

**What all crashes have in common:**
1. **Leverage amplifies destruction** — cash and no leverage = optionality during the panic
2. **The recovery always comes** — if the underlying businesses survive, assets recover. The question is whether you still hold them when they do
3. **Story changes faster than value** — markets overshoot on the way down as they overshoot on the way up
4. **Liquidity disappears at the worst time** — if you need to sell to meet obligations during a crash, you sell at the worst prices

**The script for handling market panic:**
> "This feels catastrophic. But history shows that every major crash — 1929, 1987, 2000, 2008, 2020 — recovered and made new highs. The question isn't whether the market will recover. It's whether you still own the assets when it does. If your timeline is 10+ years and the fundamentals haven't changed, holding is almost always the better decision than selling."

## Additional Historical Context

**The Great Depression (1929-1939):** The longest and deepest economic contraction in modern history. Unemployment reached 25%. Personal bankruptcies were epidemic. The lesson for investors: even a 10-year hold through the worst period would have recovered all losses — but only if you still owned the assets. Margin players were wiped out before recovery came.

**The 1970s Stagflation:** The only decade where both stocks and bonds fell together (stocks -3%/year, bonds -1%/year in real terms). The lesson: inflation-protected assets (TIPS, commodities, real estate, gold) are not optional for long-horizon wealth preservation.

**The 1987 Flash Crash:** The 22.6% single-day drop was 100% computer-driven. Portfolio insurance algos all responded to the same signals simultaneously. The lesson: in a world of algorithmic trading, human emotional fear has been replaced by algorithmic cascade risk. Individual stop-losses can trigger synchronized selling that amplifies the very drop they were meant to protect against.

**The Dot-Com Bust (2000-2002):** The Nasdaq lost 78% over 2.5 years. Companies with no revenue, no profit, and no path to either went to zero. The lesson: a great product and a great stock are different things. Stock price reflects the present value of future cash flows — not current user counts or "eyeballs."

**The 2008 Financial Crisis:** Lehman Brothers (158 years old) filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008. AIG needed an $85 billion federal bailout. The lesson: the financial institutions that caused the crisis sold the same products they were betting against. Never trust a financial product where the seller's incentives aren't aligned with yours.

**The 2020 COVID Crash:** Fastest 30% drop in history (23 days). Fastest 30% recovery in history (5 months). The lesson: for non-structural crises (pandemic, not a banking collapse or depression), the recovery can come faster than expected. Being out of the market during the recovery is the real wealth-destroying decision.

**The 2022 Rate Shock:** For the first time since 1937, both stocks and bonds fell together in the same calendar year. A 60/40 portfolio lost over 16%. TIPS, commodities, and real estate were among the few assets that held value. The lesson: "diversification" across stocks and bonds is not true diversification when both are sold off during inflation shocks.
