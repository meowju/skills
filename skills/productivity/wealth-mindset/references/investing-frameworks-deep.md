# Investing Frameworks Deep Reference

*Extended frameworks from Lynch, Greenblatt, Marks, Housel, and Carlson.*

---

## Lynch Magic Formula: The Systematic Version of "Buy Good Businesses Cheaply"

Joel Greenblatt's Magic Formula from *The Little Book That Beats the Market* (2006): rank all stocks by two metrics — earnings yield (EBIT / enterprise value) and return on capital (EBIT / net working capital + fixed assets). Buy the top 20-30 stocks, hold for one year, rebalance annually. Greenblatt back-tested 17 years (1988–2004) at 30%+ annual returns vs. S&P 500's ~10%.

**Why it works:** The formula exploits the market's short-term focus on "story" over fundamentals. Companies with high earnings yield and high ROC tend to be temporarily out-of-favor quality businesses. The annual rebalance forces you to sell winners (which have likely re-rated) and buy new out-of-favor names.

**The critical constraint:** Magic Formula only works on individual stocks if you can stomach the tracking error. In practice, low-cost ETF versions of the strategy (Constituent ETFs that screen for quality + value factors) capture most of the alpha with less volatility. Greenblatt himself recommends most people use the index fund version.

**For personal finance:** The Magic Formula is the systematic implementation of Lynch's GARP (Growth at a Reasonable Price). Both say: buy businesses with durable competitive advantages trading at reasonable prices, rebalance annually. The specific ranking system removes the emotional component.

## Lynch Ten-Bagger Checklist (The Complete Version)

Lynch hunted companies with 10x potential. The full checklist for identifying ten-baggers:

**Company characteristics:**
- Small to mid-size ($100M–$1B market cap at entry)
- Hiding in plain sight — unglamorous sector, ignored by analysts
- Only one or two products/services driving growth
- Strong brand presence where you encounter it in daily life
- Management has skin in the game (insider ownership)
- No serious competitors in a growing market

**Financial characteristics:**
- Revenue and earnings growing 20–25% annually
- Earnings per share growing faster than revenue (operating leverage)
- PEG ratio ≤ 0.75 (undervalued even for a good growth company)
- Low debt relative to equity
- Consistent earnings — no boom-bust cycles
- Cash pile building faster than they know what to do with it

**Story characteristics:**
- You can explain the business in one sentence
- The bull case doesn't require multiple things to go right simultaneously
- No need for external financing (self-funding growth)
- The company is doing something competitors aren't or can't

**The tell:** When you, as a regular person, keep encountering the product or service in daily life — long lines, full parking lots, employees who clearly believe in the product — that's the Lynch signal. Full lots + happy employees + loyal customers = a business worth holding for decades.

## Howard Marks: Second-Level Thinking and the Art of Non-Consensus Investing

Howard Marks, co-chairman of Oaktree Capital Management, wrote the definitive framework on investment psychology in *The Most Important Thing*. His core insight: **the path to above-market returns runs directly through being right when others are wrong.**

**First-level thinking:** "Company A is good — it will do well, so buy its stock."
**Second-level thinking:** "Company A is widely thought to be good — but the current price already reflects that. For me to profit, the company must do BETTER than what is already priced in. Is that likely? And am I willing to accept being wrong and losing money while waiting for the thesis to prove out?"

**Marks's key distinction:**
- First-level thinkers all agree on the same information and reach the same conclusion
- Second-level thinkers ask: what does the consensus already know? What does it NOT see? What does it overweigh?
- Being early is also wrong in the short term — Marks: *"The cid the right decision about an undervalued asset, you can be early by years."*

**Marks's most important thing:** *\"The most important thing is NOT buying assets — it's buying assets at a price that has a margin of safety. A good business at a fair price is not an investment; a fair business at a good price might be."*

**Second-level thinking checklist:**
1. What do I believe that the consensus does NOT believe?
2. What does the consensus believe that I might be wrong about?
3. Is the current price reflecting the consensus view or my non-consensus view?
4. When the consensus is wrong (as it sometimes is), will I have the conviction to hold — or will I be shaken out during the period where I'm alone?

## Morgan Housel: The Behavioral Core of Financial Success

Housel's *The Psychology of Money* (2020) is the clearest modern statement that behavior beats technical skill in wealth-building. His core framework:

**The Compounding Requires Silence principle:**
The people who become wealthy are not the people with the best investment ideas — they're the people who never interrupt the compounding process. A dollar invested in 1950 in the S&P 500 is worth ~$25,000 today with dividends reinvested. The investor who held through every crash and never sold made that return. The investor who had a "better idea" at some point along the way interrupted the compounding.

**The Room to Be Wrong principle:**
The purpose of an emergency fund and low-debt lifestyle is not to optimize returns — it's to create the psychological room to be wrong. When a drawdown comes and you're highly leveraged (margin, heavy mortgage, consumer debt), you are forced to sell at the worst moment. The person with a cash cushion and no margin exposure can afford to be wrong, wait, and see the compounding resume.

**The Saving Is More Powerful Than Returns principle:**
Morgan Housel: *"$1 saved is worth $1 earned."* The math of saving: if you earn $100K and save 20%, you need $500K invested at 10% to replace your income from capital. If you earn $100K and save 40%, you need only $250K. Reducing your cost of living by $20K/year has the same effect as earning $20K more AND investing it at 10% — but the savings is more reliable.

**Housel's behavioral rules:**
1. The best investment you can make today is the one that lets you sleep well
2. The goal is not to maximize returns — it's to maximize the probability you'll still be investing 30 years from now
3. Money's greatest value is optionality — the ability to say yes or no to opportunities
4. Getting wealthy and staying wealthy are two different skills — the second requires humility

## Charlie Carlson: The Systematic Rules-Based System

Carlson's *The Folklore of Money* framework advocates a principles-based approach that removes human judgment entirely:

**The Core Carlson System:**
1. **Equal-weight asset allocation** — Don't over-weight recent winners (the natural human bias)
2. **Annual rebalancing** — Sell what appreciated, buy what declined; systematic buy-low
3. **Low-cost implementation** — Every 1% in fees costs ~20% of terminal wealth over 30 years
4. **No forecasting required** — The system works regardless of what markets do next
5. **Tax-efficient design** — Use tax-advantaged accounts for high-turnover strategies

**Why systematic beats predictive:**
Carlson's key insight is that predicting markets is unreliable even for professionals — but following rules consistently is available to anyone. The person who commits to a systematic plan and follows it through every market condition beats the person who times the market based on predictions, because the prediction accuracy is never high enough to justify the transaction costs and behavioral taxes.

**The four rules in practice:**
1. Divide assets equally across 4–6 uncorrelated asset classes (US stocks, international stocks, bonds, REITs, commodities, cash)
2. Every January, rebalance back to equal weights — sell the best performer, buy the worst
3. Keep total portfolio costs below 0.20% (using index funds)
4. Never deviate from the system regardless of market conditions — the discipline IS the edge

## Synthesis: How These Frameworks Fit Together

**For the individual investor with limited time:**
1. Start with Carlson's systematic base (diversified, low-cost, rebalanced annually) — removes behavioral error and forecasting risk
2. Add Lynch's conviction layer: if you encounter a business in daily life that you genuinely understand and it passes the ten-bagger checklist, add it as a concentrated position (3–5% of portfolio max)
3. Apply Housel's behavior rules: build the emergency fund first, keep debt low, save more than you think you need
4. Use Marks's second-level thinking when you encounter "the next big thing" — ask what the consensus already prices in before piling in
5. Use Buffett/ Munger's mental models (moat, owner earnings, margin of safety) as the evaluation framework for any individual business decision

**The simple version:** Diversified index funds via Carlson's system, supplemented with Lynch-style observation of businesses you encounter, with Buffett/Munger's analytical discipline when evaluating a specific investment. Behavior handled by Housel's framework.
