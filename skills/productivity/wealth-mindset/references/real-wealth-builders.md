# The Real Wealth Builders

*Condensed from the wealth-mindset skill. Real wealth builders share a common set of behaviors and mental models that cut across industries, eras, and economic conditions.*

## Core Habits

- Earn more than they spend — not once, not seasonally, but as a consistent orientation
- Invest early, automatically, and simply — index funds or well-understood individual stocks
- Protect income with insurance (health, disability, life) — income is the asset that funds everything else
- Live below their means for years — lifestyle inflation is the silent wealth destroyer
- Don't discuss investments with people who haven't earned the right to an opinion
- Build systems that make right behavior automatic before willpower runs out
- Write decision principles before panic sets in — emotions erode judgment exactly when you need it most
- Compound through decades of consistent, boring behavior — the unremarkable path is the one that works

## The Pattern

The real wealth builders are not necessarily the highest earners. Many high earners end up with nothing. The builders:

1. **Earn more than they spend** — the gap between income and spending is the engine of compound growth
2. **Invest the difference automatically** — before it ever hits checking, it goes to work
3. **Stay in the game for decades** — no amount of cleverness substitutes for time in the market
4. **Protect their income** — health, disability, liability insurance as the foundation layer
5. **Keep decisions simple** — complexity creates costs (fees, errors, taxes) that compound against you

## Peter Lynch's Stock-Picking Frameworks (from Magellan Fund 1977–1990)

Lynch managed Fidelity Magellan from $18M to $14B, averaging **29.2% per year** — the best-performing mutual fund in the world during that period. His frameworks remain the clearest practical system for individual investors.

### What Made Lynch Different

Most institutional investors analyze businesses from financial statements backward. Lynch analyzed them from the customer's experience forward — and then checked the financials.

Lynch's insight: *By the time a Fidelity analyst finished reading an SEC filing about a company, Lynch had already been a customer for years. He knew whether people actually loved or hated the product before the data confirmed it.*

**This is the individual investor's structural advantage.** You interact with hundreds of businesses every year. Fidelity analysts don't.

### Lynch's Six Stock Categories

Lynch organized every stock into one of six categories — each with a different hold/sell logic:

| Category | Examples | Strategy |
|---|---|---|
| **Slow growers** | Utilities, regional banks | Hold; dividends compound |
| **Stalwarts** | Coca-Cola, P&G | Hold for decades; raise prices annually without losing customers |
| **Fast growers** | Small companies growing 20–25%/year | Buy early; verify growth is real earnings, not accounting |
| **Cyclicals** | Airlines, autos, steel | Buy when depressed, sell when peak |
| **Turnarounds** | Distressed companies recovering | High risk; needs deep conviction and understanding |
| **Asset plays** | Hidden assets (land, IP, cash pile) | Find the value the market hasn't priced yet |

### The Two-Minute Drill (Before Buying Any Stock)

Lynch asked five questions in two minutes. If you can't answer them, you don't understand the business:

1. **Can I describe the business in one sentence?** If you can't, you don't understand it.
2. **Is revenue or earnings growing at least 10%/year?** Without growth, it's a slow grower at most.
3. **Are earnings per share growing** (not just revenue)? Revenue up, earnings down = trouble.
4. **Is the P/E reasonable relative to growth rate?** GARP rule: PEG ≤ 1.0 is potentially undervalued.
5. **Would I hold this through a 30% drawdown?** If no, you don't have conviction — sell now.

### GARP: Growth at a Reasonable Price

Lynch coined GARP — the middle path between pure growth (too expensive) and pure value (often cheap for good reasons).

- PEG ≤ 1.0: potentially undervalued
- PEG ≤ 0.75: likely undervalued
- PEG > 1.5: paying too much for the growth even if the business is good

The goal: *buy good companies growing 15–25%/year at a P/E that doesn't exceed their growth rate.*

### Ten Baggers: The Multi-Bagger Hunt

A "ten bagger" is a stock that returns 10x your investment. Lynch defined it from baseball (four bases = a home run; ten bases = something extraordinary).

Lynch's biggest winners:
- **Fannie Mae**: 10,000%+ return — bought when unloved, grew faster than expected
- **Ford**: $199M profit for Magellan
- **Dunkin' Donuts**: Bought because Lynch was a regular customer and noticed the lines
- **Philip Morris**: $111M profit — bought the "vice" stock everyone dismissed

**The ten-bagger pattern:** Unloved business + visible long-term growth tailwind + Lynch's own customer data + financial confirmation = conviction to hold through volatility.

### Lynch on the Individual Investor's Edge

> *"Your investor's edge is not something you get from Wall Street experts. It's something you already have. You can outperform experts by investing in companies and industries you already understand."*

> *"Far more money has been lost by investors preparing for corrections or trying to anticipate corrections than has been lost in the corrections themselves."*

> *"Know what you don't know. That's more valuable than being right."*

> *"The most expensive stock on Wall Street is a 'stock that has gone down' rather than a 'stock that has gone up.' People fall in love with the story of why it will recover instead of asking why it went down."*

> *"Invest in a business any fool can run — because eventually some fool will."*

### The Sell Discipline (Often Ignored)

Lynch was ruthless about selling:
- **The story changed** — the business fundamentals deteriorated
- **It reached your price target** — you called it correctly, take the win
- **You need capital for a better opportunity** — better opportunity wins

Otherwise: *leave it alone.* The individual investor's greatest structural advantage is that they don't have to answer to clients redeeming during a drawdown. Use that freedom.

### Lynch's Biggest Mental Model: Invest in What You Know

The individual investor encounters dozens of businesses daily — restaurants, retailers, software tools, hospitals, banks. Most analyzing professionals work in offices reading filings. The individual has a data advantage they don't have: **lived experience of whether people actually use, love, and repurchase products.**

The framework:
1. Notice a product you or people around you repeatedly choose, repurchase, or recommend
2. Ask: *Is this a public company?* (Most are.)
3. Check: revenue/earnings growth ≥ 10%/year, EPS growing, PEG ≤ 1.0
4. If yes → you have an information edge. Investigate further.

This is not tips from friends. This is systematic attention to your own consumption patterns as a stock-screening tool.

### Buffett's Circle of Competence + Lynch's "Know It"

Buffett and Lynch agree on one principle above all others: **only buy what you understand.** Buffett says "circle of competence." Lynch says "invest in what you know." The same principle, 50 years apart.

Buffett's filter for buying a business (apply to buying a stock):
1. Can a smart junior analyst understand it? If it needs a genius, skip it.
2. Does it have durable earnings with no or low leverage?
3. Does it have a competitive moat lasting 10+ years?
4. Is the price reasonable relative to intrinsic value?
5. Would I be comfortable if the market closed for 10 years?

**If all five are yes → you have conviction. Conviction is what lets you hold through a 30% drawdown.**

### The Common Thread

Every real wealth builder — Lynch, Buffett, Marks, Lynch's own ten-baggers — shared traits:

- **Conviction from understanding, not tips** — they knew why they owned each position
- **Patience as the actual edge** — the market rewards those who stay in the game
- **Simplicity over sophistication** — boring, consistent, cheap beats complex and clever over time
- **Written decision rules** — rules written in calm markets govern behavior in panicked ones
- **Income greater than spending** — the gap is the actual wealth engine

## Connecting the Builders

These frameworks are not contradictory — they're complementary:

- **Lynch → individual stock selection:** "Invest in what you know, using the six categories and Two-Minute Drill"
- **Buffett → valuation and moat analysis:** "Only buy businesses with durable competitive advantage inside your circle of competence"
- **Marks → second-level thinking:** "What do I know that the market doesn't? If nothing, I'm doing first-level thinking"
- **Munger → inversion:** "Ask what guarantees failure, then don't do that"

The combination: *You know something through direct experience → check if it meets Lynch's criteria → evaluate the moat with Buffett's framework → ask Marks's second-level question → invert with Munger → hold for 10 years.*

This is not sophistication. This is a coherent system built from four minds that all arrived at the same truth from different directions.
