# The Grit Framework: Full Reference

*Source: Angela Duckworth (Grit, 2016), Carol Dweck (Mindset, 2006), Walter Mischel (marshmallow test, Stanford 1970s)*

---

## Core Definition

Angela Duckworth defines **grit** as *passion and perseverance for long-term goals* — not talent, IQ, or connections. Her Grit Scale became one of the most replicated findings in modern psychology, predicting achievement better than any other metric across West Point, National Spelling Bee, sales teams, and military training.

**Two components:**
1. **Consistency of interest** — Do you keep returning to the same goals over years, not weeks?
2. **Persistence of effort** — Do you maintain effort even when progress is invisible?

---

## Duckworth's Framework

### The "Hard Thing Rule"
(From her physicist father)
- Everyone does one hard thing every day
- No opting out when it gets uncomfortable
- You choose the hard thing — don't have it imposed on you

### Grit Is Not About Burning Out
Gritty people are marathoners who pace themselves. The key is *long-term stamina*, not short-term intensity.

### The "Four Ps" for Developing Grit

1. **Purpose** — Your work connects to something that matters beyond yourself
2. **Practice** — Deliberate, focused improvement every day (not just experience)
3. **Progress** — Track small wins; visible progress sustains motivation
4. **Priority** — Protect your time; say no to good opportunities to say yes to great ones

## Growth Mindset (Carol Dweck, Stanford)

**Two mindsets:**

**Fixed mindset:** "I am talented or I'm not. Failure defines me." → Avoids challenges, sees effort as fruitless, collapses under setbacks.

**Growth mindset:** "I can develop my abilities. Failure is data." → Embraces challenges, sees effort as the path to mastery, persists through setbacks.

### Wealth-Building Application
- Reframe every financial setback as *feedback*, not defeat
- Ask "What is this teaching me?" instead of "Why am I unlucky?"
- Say "I'm learning to handle money" instead of "I'm not good with money"

Dweck's research shows that a single shift — from "I can't" to "I can't yet" — changes behavior and outcomes dramatically.

---

## Delayed Gratification (Walter Mischel, Stanford)

Mischel's marshmallow test (1970s) showed children who could delay gratification had better life outcomes decades later. The ability to delay is *learnable*, not fixed.

### How to Build Delay Tolerance

1. **Visualize the outcome** — Imagine the future reward vividly
2. **Change the frame** — See the tempting short-term option as less appealing
3. **Create friction** — Make the bad choice harder to access
4. **Reframe the wait** — "I'm choosing to wait" feels different from "I have to wait"

---

## The Wealth-Builder's Identity Stack

Duckworth, Dweck, and Mischel's work converge on one principle: **your financial results are downstream of who you believe yourself to be with money**.

- "I am someone who builds" — not someone who earns
- "I am patient with money" — not someone who chases
- "I learn from losses" — not someone who is defeated by them

Grit, growth mindset, and delayed gratification are the exact mental infrastructure that makes compound wealth possible. The wealthy aren't just smarter or luckier; they are the ones who kept going when it was hard, who saw failure as tuition, and who could wait for the payoff that never came to those who quit.
