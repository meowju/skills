## The Three Trust Types That Matter Most

### 1. Revocable Living Trust — The Foundation Plan

A revocable living trust (RLT) is a legal container holding title to your assets. You (the grantor) own the assets and retain the right to revoke or change it at any time during your life.

**What it does:**
- **Avoids probate** — Assets in the trust pass to beneficiaries without court involvement. Probate is public, expensive ($3,000-$50,000+), and slow (6 months to 3 years). The RLT bypasses all of it.
- **Manages incapacity** — If you become mentally incapacitated, the successor trustee (you named in the document) takes over seamlessly. No court guardianship proceedings, no loss of control.
- **Coordinates asset transfer** — You can specify exactly how assets are distributed and when (e.g., "remain in trust for the benefit of my children until age 30").

**What it doesn't do:** An RLT provides NO protection from creditors or lawsuits. Because you retain control, the assets are still legally yours for liability purposes. This is the most common misunderstanding — people think a "living trust" protects their home from lawsuits; it doesn't.

**The practical setup:** Fund the trust by retitling assets (bank accounts, real estate, investment accounts) in the name of the trust. The trust owns them. You manage them as trustee. This is administrative, not complicated.

**The script:** "The revocable trust is your operating system for wealth — it organizes how your assets are held and transferred, avoiding the court system entirely. Everyone with a mortgage, savings, or investments should have one."

### 2. Irrevocable Trust — The Asset Protection Heavy Artillery

Once funded, an irrevocable trust cannot be changed or revoked without the consent of all beneficiaries. This sounds like a downside — and it is, in terms of flexibility — but it's precisely what makes it powerful.

**The asset protection mechanism:** When you transfer assets to an irrevocable trust, those assets are no longer legally owned by you. A creditor who wins a judgment against you cannot reach assets inside an properly structured irrevocable trust (in most states with favorable trust law). The trust — not you — owns the assets.

**The estate tax benefit:** Assets in an irrevocable trust are removed from your taxable estate. For federal estate tax purposes (currently exemption of ~$13.6M per person in 2024, scheduled to drop to ~$7M in 2026 without Congressional action), trust-owned assets escape the estate tax calculation entirely.

**The tradeoff:** You lose direct access to the assets. You cannot be the trustee (typically) and you cannot revoke the trust. Once the assets are in, they're governed by the trust document's terms, not your wishes. This is a permanent structural decision.

**Best uses:**
- Medicaid planning (the 5-year lookback rule means assets transferred to an irrevocable trust are counted for Medicaid eligibility purposes for 5 years)
- Protecting assets from a known litigation risk (lawsuits, business liability)
- Special needs planning for disabled beneficiaries
- Generation-skipping transfer (GST trusts to pass wealth to grandchildren tax-efficiently)

**State considerations:** South Dakota, Nevada, and Delaware have the strongest trust laws and best creditor protections. Many high-net-worth individuals establish trusts in these states even if they live elsewhere.

### 3. Grantor Trust / Intentionally Defective Grantor Trust (IDGT) — The Compounding Engine

This is the most sophisticated trust type and the one most directly applicable to wealth building and business owners.

**How a grantor trust works:** The trust is a separate tax entity, but the grantor (you) pays the income taxes on trust income. This is unusual — normally a trust pays its own taxes. But from the grantor's perspective, paying the trust's taxes is like making a tax-free gift to the trust — you're using personal funds to reduce the trust's tax burden, allowing more assets to compound tax-free inside.

**The IDGT specifically:** An Intentionally Defective Grantor Trust is "defective" because it is not a separate tax entity for income tax purposes (the grantor pays taxes), but it IS a separate entity for estate tax purposes (assets are removed from the estate). This is the powerful combination: you pay taxes on the growth, reducing the trust's tax burden, while the assets compound outside the estate.

**The classic IDGT transaction (sale transaction):**
1. Create an IDGT (trust is the grantor taxpayer)
2. Sell assets to the trust in exchange for a promissory note at the IRS AFR rate (Applicable Federal Rate, currently ~4-5%)
3. The trust's assets (e.g., a business, real estate, stock portfolio) grow at a rate faster than the AFR
4. The trust sells, compounds, reinvests — the loan interest goes back to you as income (at ordinary income rates, but you've already paid taxes on it via the grantor trust mechanism)
5. The growth above the loan repayment stays in the trust, never subject to estate tax

**Example:** You own a business worth $5M. You sell a minority interest to an IDGT for a $5M promissory note at 4% IRS AFR. The trust's business interest grows at 15%/year. The trust generates $550K/year in value above the interest cost. That $550K compounds in the trust — outside your estate, outside your income tax return. Over 20 years, this can transfer tens of millions of dollars tax-free.

**The script:** "A grantor trust is a container where you pay the taxes so the container can grow faster. An IDGT takes that and adds a loan mechanism — you sell the trust an asset at fair market value, they pay you back with a fixed-interest note, and all the growth above that rate stays in the trust forever, free of estate tax."

## Trust Fundamentals: Structure and Operations

**The parties:**
- **Grantor** — The person who creates and funds the trust; can be a beneficiary (for revocable) or not (for irrevocable)
- **Trustee** — The legal owner and manager of trust assets. Has fiduciary duty to beneficiaries. Can be a person or a corporate trustee (bank trust department). For revocable trusts, you can be your own trustee.
- **Beneficiary** — The person who benefits from the trust; receives distributions per trust terms

**Key provisions:**
- **Spendthrift clause** — Prevents a beneficiary from selling their trust interest to creditors. Protects beneficiaries from their own poor decisions and from lawsuits.
- **Decant provision** — Allows a trustee to distribute assets to a different trust (useful if state law changes or the original trust becomes outdated). Not available in all states.
- **Power to amend** — Some trusts保留 the right to amend (revocable trusts always; some irrevocable trusts include this as a negotiated provision with beneficiaries).

**State domicile matters:** The state where the trust is established (not where you live) determines which state's trust laws govern. South Dakota, Nevada, and Delaware are the top jurisdictions because they have:
- No state income tax for trusts
- Long statutes of limitation for creditor claims
- Strong protection against courts piercing trust structures
- Experienced trust attorneys and corporate trustees

## Estate Planning: The Wealthy Act Before They Have To

Estate planning is the process of deciding what happens to your assets during life, at death, and for future generations. The wealthy treat it as an ongoing system, not a one-time event.

**The foundational documents:**
1. **Revocable Living Trust** — Who gets what, and when; incapacity plan
2. **Pour-Over Will** — Catches any assets not in the trust and pours them into it
3. **Durable Power of Attorney** — Someone can manage your financial affairs if you can't
4. **Healthcare Directive** — Someone can make medical decisions if you can't

**The estate tax problem:** The federal estate tax currently takes 40% of assets above the exemption (~$13.6M per person in 2024). Without planning, married couples effectively lose $20M+ to estate tax over their lifetimes. For business owners and high earners, this is a massive wealth transfer inefficiency.

**The spousal lifetime access trust (SLAT):** One popular structure for married couples: one spouse creates an irrevocable trust for the other's benefit (the beneficiaries can include children). Assets transferred to the SLAT are removed from the grantor's estate. The beneficiary-spouse gets distributions during their lifetime (maintaining some access), and the assets ultimately pass to children. The "defect" is that if the marriage dissolves, those assets may not be protected (the anti-Dower laws of certain states complicate this).

**The generation-skipping trust (GST):** Designed to pass wealth to grandchildren, skipping one generation. Uses the generation-skipping transfer (GST) exemption — each person has a separate ~$13.6M exemption specifically for transfers to grandchildren or more remote generations. If you have significant wealth, allocating GST exemption to trusts for grandchildren can save enormous estate tax over multiple generations.

## The Practical Estate Planning Sequence

For someone building wealth who hasn't done formal estate planning yet:

**Step 1 — Will and Healthcare Directive ($500-$1,500):** These basic documents cover the basics. Any attorney can draft these. Without them, your state intestacy laws determine who gets your assets — and state law rarely matches your preferences.

**Step 2 — Revocable Living Trust ($2,000-$5,000):** For most people with a home, retirement accounts, and meaningful savings, this is the core document. Retitle assets to the trust and the probate avoidance kicks in.

**Step 3 — Irrevocable trust for asset protection ($3,000-$10,000):** Once you have significant assets (business owners, physicians, anyone with a known liability risk), move specific assets into irrevocable trusts. Prioritize business interests and investment real estate.

**Step 4 — IDGT / wealth transfer trust (consultation required):** When you have $5M+ in assets, a meeting with an estate planning attorney focused on IDGT strategies can yield transformative results. The cost ($10,000-$30,000 for complex structures) is a fraction of the estate tax savings.

## The Script for "Estate Planning Sounds Like Something for Old People"

> "It's exactly the opposite — estate planning works best when you're young and healthy, because you can use all your estate tax exemptions now and let the assets compound for decades inside the trust structure. The 40% estate tax is the largest single wealth transfer tax in the country. If you have a $2M estate today, a properly structured trust could save your family $800,000 in taxes over your lifetime and theirs. It's not about death — it's about keeping more of what you build."
