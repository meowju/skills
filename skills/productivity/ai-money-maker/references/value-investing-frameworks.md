# Value Investing Frameworks: From Graham to Marks

*Research summary — applicable to both ai-money-maker and wealth-mindset.*

---

## Warren Buffett: Moats, Compounding, and Circle of Competence

**Core thesis:** The best businesses have an economic moat — a competitive advantage that's durable, not temporary. A wide moat means the business can hold pricing power, resist competitors, and generate consistent returns over decades.

**Buffett's investment criteria (simplified):**
1. **Understand the business** — can you explain it in one sentence? If you can't, you don't understand it well enough.
2. **Favorable long-term economics** — high return on equity, minimal debt, consistent earnings power.
3. **Honest, capable management** — integrity matters more than brilliance.
4. **Buy at a reasonable price, not a cheap price** — quality beats cheapness. A great business at a fair price > a mediocre business at a cheap price.

**The compounding math:** Buffett's famous example — a $10,000 investment at 12% annual return becomes $1M in ~40 years. At 15%, it hits $1M in ~33 years. The difference of 3 percentage points costs 7 years of waiting. This is why Buffett says "the most important investment is the one you don't sell."

**Moat examples:**
- Brand (Coca-Cola, Apple)
- Network effects (American Express, Visa)
- Cost advantage (Costco, Geico)
- Regulatory moat (utility companies)
- Switching costs (Microsoft, Adobe)

**Circle of competence:** Only invest in businesses you understand. Buffett famously avoided tech for decades — not because he couldn't learn it, but because insurance and consumer brands were more legible to him. When he entered tech, it was Apple (a consumer brand with a moat, not a pure tech bet).

## Joel Greenblatt: The Magic Formula

**What it is:** A systematic two-step ranking of all stocks:

1. **Rank by earnings yield** (earnings ÷ price — higher is better)
2. **Rank by return on invested capital** (higher is better)
3. **Combine rankings** — buy top 20-30 stocks, rebalance annually

**Why it works:** Combines Graham's cheapness criterion with a quality filter. Cheap + quality is the combination most likely to survive the test of time.

**The catch:** Greenblatt himself notes performance drops after ~2001 (more institutional competition in small-cap space). It still beats the market, but not by as much. Markets are more efficient now.

**For ai-money-maker purposes:** The formula is a template for thinking about business quality AND price simultaneously. When evaluating any monetization opportunity (buying a business, investing in a project, evaluating a partnership), ask: is this cheap relative to what I'm getting, AND is the underlying quality high?

## Howard Marks: Second-Level Thinking

**The key insight:** Most investors think at the first level. The money is in thinking one level deeper.

- **First-level:** "Company X is doing well → buy it."
- **Second-level:** "Everyone knows Company X is doing well → the price already reflects this. Is it priced HIGHER than its value? What do I know that the market might not be pricing in?"

**Marks on risk:**
- Risk is not volatility. A 5% bond can be riskier than a 30% stock if the bond is more likely to default.
- Risk = probability of permanent capital loss.
- The goal is return without risk — not high return with high risk.

**For ai-money-maker purposes:** Before any business or investment decision, ask: *What do I know about this opportunity that the market doesn't?* If the answer is "nothing," you're doing first-level thinking. Either build genuine insight, or buy the index.

## The Benjamin Graham Foundation: Price vs. Value

**The original insight:** The market quotes prices, not values. Price is what you pay; value is what you get. They are only loosely correlated in the short term — but tightly correlated over time.

**Mr. Market:** Graham's parable — imagine your business partner is an emotional wreck who offers to buy you out at wildly different prices depending on his mood. Sometimes he's euphoric (overpriced); sometimes he's terrified (underpriced). The rational move: check the fundamentals, then act accordingly — buy when he's cheap, sell when he's expensive, ignore him when he's neither.

**Margin of Safety:** Never pay full value. Buy with a buffer. If a bridge is rated for 30,000 lbs, don't drive 30,000-lb trucks across it. If a business is worth $100, don't pay $100 — pay $70. The 30% discount is your margin of safety for being wrong.

**The wealth application:** Whether acquiring a business, investing in a stock, or evaluating a major purchase — always ask: am I paying below fair value, with a margin that protects me if I'm wrong? If you routinely pay full value, your errors compound against you. If you routinely demand a margin of safety, your errors have room to breathe.

## Common Pitfalls for ai-money-maker Practitioners

1. **Confusing cheapness with quality** — a failing business can be cheap forever. Buy quality at a fair price, not low quality at a cheap price.
2. **Ignoring the time cost of capital** — holding cash in a declining business costs more than the apparent "savings" from buying cheap.
3. **Not building a circle of competence** — the best investments come from deep knowledge of a specific domain. Buffett's best decisions came from industries he understood thoroughly.
4. **Confusing price movements with value changes** — a stock dropping 30% doesn't mean it got 30% cheaper. The value may have dropped 40%. Check the fundamentals, not the chart.
5. **Over-optimizing for the formula** — Greenblatt's Magic Formula works, but imperfectly and inconsistently. The underlying principle (buy quality cheap) is more durable than the specific ranking system.
