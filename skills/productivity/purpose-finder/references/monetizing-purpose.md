# Monetizing Your Purpose

This reference covers the full content for the condensed "## Framework: Monetizing Your Purpose" section in SKILL.md.

## The Three Core Models

### The Craftsman Model — Deep Skill, Premium Pricing

You become genuinely excellent at something difficult and rare, then charge accordingly. This is the freelance, consulting, and bespoke service model.

**Mechanism:** Build career capital in a domain → position yourself as the person who solves a specific hard problem → raise prices as your reputation grows.

**Best for:** Skills requiring deep expertise — writing, design, engineering, medicine, law, finance, therapy, music, acting, architecture.

**Key insight:** You do not need to love every aspect of this model. You need to care enough about the craft to get genuinely excellent at it. The money follows the excellence, not the passion.

### The Audience Model — Knowledge + Platform

You build public expertise in a domain, attract people who share your interest, and monetize through teaching, writing, speaking, or consulting.

**Mechanism:** Give away your best thinking for free (builds trust and audience) → offer paid products, courses, coaching, or communities → diversify revenue.

**Best for:** People energized by sharing knowledge more than doing individual client work. Requires consistency and patience — audience building takes 12–24 months before meaningful traction.

**Key insight:** Most people who fail at this model quit at month 3 and call it a verdict. The people who succeed usually show up consistently for 18 months before seeing real results.

### The Creator Economy Model — Content + Community

You build an audience around shared values or interests and monetize through subscriptions, merchandise, events, or platform revenue.

**Mechanism:** Create consistently in a niche you genuinely care about → build community (not just an audience) → diversify revenue across multiple streams.

**Best for:** People energized by building community, not just delivering individual services. Requires comfort with visibility and consistency.

## The 1000 True Fans Framework

Kevin Kelly's "1000 true fans" concept: you do not need millions of people. You need 1,000 people who genuinely love what you do and will pay $100/year for access to more of it.

**That is $100,000/year** — sustainable for one person, life-changing for most.

**Conditions:**
- **True fan:** Someone who will buy everything you make, recommend you to everyone they know, and feel genuinely richer for having your work in their life — not a casual follower
- You do not need millions of followers. You need a small group of people who care intensely
- The implication: stop trying to appeal to everyone. Purpose-driven work that earns money usually starts by being deeply useful to a specific group

## The Audience First Fallacy

The most common mistake: building something with no audience and then hoping people show up. The "build it and they will come" myth destroys more purpose-driven projects than any other cause.

**The alternative is the audience-first approach:** start in public, build a relationship with people who care about what you care about, and then create the thing they need.

| Traditional | Audience-First |
|-------------|----------------|
| Build the thing | Find the people |
| Try to find customers | Learn what they need |
| Hope they come | Build the thing for them |

## The Sequencing Problem

Many people get the sequence backwards:

**Wrong sequence:** know purpose perfectly → build something in secret → find an audience

**Right sequence:**
1. Start doing the work in public
2. Audience reveals what matters
3. Purpose clarifies through the feedback loop
4. Monetization becomes possible when you know what people actually need

> **You do not find your purpose and then build an audience. You build an audience and your purpose clarifies through the relationship.**

This is uncomfortable for people who want certainty before they commit. But people who build in public find their direction faster than people who build in private.

## Monetization Without Selling Out

The fear that monetization corrupts purpose is real — but only when the monetization model rewards something different from the purpose.

**Warning signs:**
- Creating content that doesn't resonate because you're optimizing for clicks
- Offering services that drain you because they pay well
- Performing a version of yourself that feels false because it sells better

**The antidote:** Define what you will not do for money before you start making any. Write it down. Purpose that can't survive contact with money wasn't purpose — it was a hobby. But purpose that sells out to survive isn't purpose either — it's survival.

**The goal:** Work that matters to you, that other people find valuable enough to pay for, that you can sustain.

## What to Do When You're Not Ready to Charge

Many people paralyze themselves believing they need to be "expert enough" before charging. The trap: you don't need to be the world's best expert — you need to be one step ahead of the person who can't figure it out themselves.

**The progression:**
1. Give things away for free (builds audience and trust)
2. Start charging small amounts (validates the offer)
3. Raise prices as expertise and reputation grow
4. Eventually earn a full living from work you would do anyway

**Why this works:** The person who just learned something often teaches it better than the expert who's forgotten what it was like to not know. The beginner's empathy is a teaching asset, not a liability.

## The Three Monetization Mistakes

**1. Starting with the product instead of the person.**
Build the thing, hope people come. The correct sequence: find the person with the problem → build the thing they need → monetize. Not the reverse.

**2. Pricing too low too long.**
Underpricing signals underconfidence and devalues your work. Price at a level that feels slightly uncomfortable — that's usually closer to the right number than what feels "fair."

**3. Diversifying too early.**
Before you have product-market fit, multiple revenue streams分散 your attention and prevent you from understanding what actually works. Focus on one model until it works, then add.

## The Cash Flow Reality

Most purpose-driven projects fail not because the idea was bad but because the person ran out of money before the audience could develop.

**Solutions:**
- Keep your cost structure low during the building phase
- Have a income bridge (part-time work, consulting) that funds the purpose work
- Accept that the monetization will come 12–36 months after the work starts — plan accordingly

The craftsman model has the shortest path to income (you can start charging immediately). The audience and creator models require a runway.
