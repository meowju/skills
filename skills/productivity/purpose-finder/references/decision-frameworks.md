## The OODA Loop Completion (Truncated from SKILL.md)

**"I keep looping without deciding":** OODA — Observe, Orient, Decide, Act. Most people are stuck in the orient phase. Force a decision and get a real outcome to react to.

The correct OODA for purpose-finding:
1. **Observe** — What is actually happening in your life right now? Not what you think should be happening.
2. **Orient** — What does this information mean given your history, values, and the current environment? This is where most people get stuck — they over-orient.
3. **Decide** — Pick one direction, not the perfect direction.
4. **Act** — Execute and get real feedback. The real world is the only test that matters.

> The person who gets feedback faster wins. If you're always gathering more information before deciding, someone who decides and reacts will know more than you by the time you're ready.

**When someone says "I need more information before deciding":**
> Information accelerates decision quality only after you've decided and are acting. Before that, more information is usually a way to avoid deciding. What's the minimum you need to know to move forward?

## The Reversal Test — Detecting False Purpose

The Reversal Test (from the philosopher LaRue) is a technique for cutting through false purpose declarations:

When someone says "My purpose is X," ask: **"Would you still want X if you couldn't be recognized for it?"**

This reveals whether the stated purpose is about the activity itself or about the recognition it brings.

- "My purpose is to write" → "What if no one ever read your writing?"
  - If the answer is immediate distress → the purpose is actually recognition, not writing
  - If the answer is "I'd still write, just differently" → eudaimonic territory
  
- "My purpose is to help people" → "What if no one knew you were helping them?"
  - If the answer is frustration → the purpose is status, not service
  - If the answer is "I'd find different ways to help" → genuine purpose

**The deeper version:** "Would you still pursue this if you had to do it in secret, alone, with no possibility of anyone ever knowing?" If yes: real. If no: the purpose is partly social validation and that social validation is doing most of the motivational work.

## Eisenhower Matrix as Purpose Filter

The Eisenhower matrix (urgent/important) is usually taught as a productivity tool. But it's also a purpose filter:

1. **Quadrant II (Important, Not Urgent)** — This is where purpose lives. If someone's entire life is in Quadrant I (urgent everything) or Quadrant IV (neither), they have no purpose practice. The question is: what's in your Quadrant II?

2. **The Purpose Quadrant Test:** Take your current task list. Ask: "Which of these would I do even if no one knew?" The overlap between that question and your actual list reveals how much of your life is purpose-driven vs. validation-driven.

## The 10-10-10 Rule (Susan Jeffers)

Susan Jeffers' 10-10-10 framework: How will you feel about this decision in 10 minutes, 10 months, 10 years?

This is useful for people who are paralyzed by short-term emotion:
- **10 minutes:** The anxiety is usually temporary regardless of which direction you choose
- **10 months:** The decision will have produced real information — you'll know more than you do now
- **10 years:** Will this matter in 10 years? (Usually, the things that feel catastrophic in the moment don't matter at 10 years)

**Use when:** Someone is in acute decision anxiety — the 10-10-10 breaks the abstract future into tangible time horizons.

## The Two-Cardinal-Virtues Test

From Aristotle via modern philosophy: two virtues are foundational — **courage** and **temperance** (the ability to delay gratification). Purpose often hides behind their absence.

Ask: **"What's the courageous thing to do here? What's the temperate thing to do here?"**

The direction is usually not in the dramatic choice — it's in the combination of courage (doing the thing that scares you) and temperance (not doing the thing that gratifies you but harms you).

Many people with no clear purpose are actually people who know what they should do but are avoiding either the courage cost or the temperance cost. The purpose question is often: "What am I avoiding?"

## Compounding Decision Framework: The Purpose Version of Compound Interest

Purpose compounds. Small daily commitments to a direction compound over years into something unrecognizable from the starting point.

**The math:** If you dedicate 30 minutes a day to a skill or practice that compounds (learning, creative work, physical practice), after 10 years you will have invested ~1,825 hours. That is 1,825 hours of compounding interest on whatever your direction is.

The enemy of compounding is switching. Every time you change directions before compounding, you start the interest clock over at zero.

**The question:** What one direction could you commit to for 10 years that would compound meaningfully? Not "what's the perfect direction" — what direction, if you stuck with it, would produce something?
