# Designing Your Life — Bill Burnett & Dave Evans

Full reference for the Designing Your Life methodology from *Designing Your Life* (Burnett & Evans, 2016), Stanford d.school.

## Overview

Bill Burnett and Dave Evans developed a design methodology for life and career at Stanford's d.school. Unlike traditional career planning (define a goal, execute it), their approach treats life design as an **iterative design problem**: generate many options, build prototypes, test with real experience, and update based on what you learn.

Core premise: most career dissatisfaction comes from having too few options, not from making the wrong choice among the options you have. The methodology's first job is to generate more viable options.

## The Three Life Designs

Write three distinct 5-year life designs. Each is a brief narrative description, not a detailed plan.

**Design 1: The Life You Have Now**
- Current trajectory extended forward 5 years
- What happens if you keep going as you are?
- Most people don't write this honestly — they write a polished version of the present

**Design 2: The Life with One Lever Pulled**
- One significant change: career pivot, relationship change, location move, health intervention
- Only one lever — the thing that's been nagging you
- This is usually the "obvious" alternative people already half-consider

**Design 3: The Scary One**
- The option you'd choose if all safety nets disappeared
- No backup plan, no safety valve, no "just in case"
- People consistently find that writing this reveals what they already know but have been avoiding

> *"The goal is not to pick the right life. The goal is to design lives you could grow into."*

**Rating each design:**
- Would I enjoy the daily work? (not the prestige, not the outcome — the day-to-day)
- Would the world be better if this life existed? (genuine contribution, not just impact)
- Is this realistic? (given resources, constraints, current position)

The third question filters out pure fantasy. The first two often reveal that the scary option scores higher than the current life.

## The Good Time Journal

Two-week daily tracking of when you feel most alive:

| Signal | What to record |
|--------|----------------|
| **Flow** | Lost track of time, fully absorbed |
| **Energy** | Left energized, not drained |
| **Attention** | Captured your interest, even briefly |

At day 14, review patterns. What type of activity recurs across all three columns? The intersection of flow + energy + attention is your direction signal.

**Common mistake:** Recording what you think should be fulfilling rather than what actually is. If the journal is full of "should" entries, stop and record what you actually did today — including the 20 minutes spent on something pointless.

## The Life Design Interview

Before committing to any path, study how others have designed theirs.

**Whom to interview:** Three people whose *decisions* intrigue you. Not people you want to become (outcome envy is useless), but people whose *choices* interest you — the person who pivoted careers at 45, the one who turned a hobby into a livelihood, the one who chose financial simplicity.

**What to ask:**
- What does a typical Tuesday look like for you?
- What did this path cost you that doesn't appear on a resume?
- Would you make the same choice again?
- What do you know now that you wish you'd known when you started?

The goal is **unglamorized data**. Specific tradeoffs, real costs, actual day-to-day reality — not their polished narrative. People are usually honest about the costs if you ask directly.

## The Odyssey Plan (5-Year Template)

For each of the three life designs, fill in:

```
Current reality: Where am I now?
Ideal reality: Where would I be in this design?
Fallback reality: If this doesn't work, what's the landing strip?
Resources: time, money, skills, connections I have
Identity: Who do I become in this life?
Return: What do I get back — not money, but quality of life, growth, contribution
```

The fallback reality question is underused. Every good life design has an exit ramp — something you can recover to if the experiment fails. Knowing the exit ramp makes the jump easier.

## Prototyping

The most critical step most people skip: **test with real experience before committing.**

**Prototype levels (in order of fidelity):**
1. **Mind view** — talk to people doing this, read about it, research deeply
2. **Body view** — shadow for 2 hours, be in the environment, observe without participating
3. **Action view** — volunteer, assist, do actual work in that domain

Most people stop at level 1 and call it research. Level 2 reveals things level 1 misses entirely. Level 3 is where the body gives feedback the mind can't compute.

The rule: if you can't prototype a career direction in 2 hours of actual presence, you're not ready to commit to it.

**Prototype conversation starter:**
*"I'm exploring a potential career direction and I'd like to spend a couple hours observing what you actually do. Would you be open to that for 2 hours sometime in the next few weeks?"*

People say yes more often than you'd expect, especially if you're specific about the time commitment and curious about their experience.

## Common Life Design Failures

**Failure 1: Falling in love with Design 1**
The life you have now is familiar and safe. Writing it as an option can make it look better than it is because you're comparing a real, experienced life against two hypothetical ones. Counter: write Design 1 last, or write it as honestly as possible (the bad parts too).

**Failure 2: Treating the first prototype as the final answer**
The methodology is designed to be iterative. If the first prototype fails, that's data — not a verdict. Update the design and prototype again.

**Failure 3: Generating only one design**
If you only write one option, you're not designing — you're planning. The value is in the comparison. Three designs forces honest evaluation against alternatives.

**Failure 4: Skipping the scary one**
People write a "moderately different" Design 3 because the truly scary option feels too risky. But the whole point of Design 3 is to surface what you'd choose if fear weren't the constraint. If you soften it, you defeat the purpose.

**Failure 5: Not updating after testing**
Life design produces data. The most common mistake: collect data from a prototype, then ignore it because it contradicts what you hoped. Update the design, don't dismiss the data.

## Integration with Flow-Based Purpose Discovery

Designing Your Life and Csikszentmihalyi's flow research are complementary:

- **Flow** tells you the *mechanism* — what conditions produce absorption (challenge-skill balance, immediate feedback, clear goals)
- **Life design** tells you the *method* — how to generate and test options to find the right challenge

The Good Time Journal (life design) and the Flow Log (flow framework) track the same signals from different angles. Used together, they produce more reliable direction data than either alone.
