## The ACT Values Framework

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT, pronounced "act") makes a sharp distinction between **values** and **goals**, and this distinction is one of the most practically useful tools in purpose work.

**Goals** are specific outcomes you can achieve and cross off. When you achieve them, the motivation that drove you toward them typically fades. "Get promoted" — once achieved, the drive to maintain it is often weaker than the drive that got you there.

**Values** are qualities of engagement that you keep moving toward — never completed, always alive. "Be integrity-driven in my work" — you cannot achieve this and stop. It is a direction, not a destination. When you act in integrity, you feel it immediately — and you can act this way again tomorrow, and the next day, indefinitely.

The distinction explains a common pattern: people who achieve their goals (the promotion, the income, the house) and find emptiness afterward. The goal was real but finite. The person who finds purpose moves in a valued direction — and never runs out of road because the direction is unbounded.

## The Values Sort (60 Seconds)

Complete this without editing yourself:

**"What I care about most, in roughly this order..."**

Write 5–10 items. The discipline: write **qualities of engagement**, not achievements and not people.

| Instead of this | Write this |
|---|---|
| "My family" | "Genuine presence with the people I love" |
| "Get promoted" | "Be respected for the quality of my work" |
| "Make more money" | "Have the autonomy to make my own decisions about my time" |
| "Help people" | "Be of genuine service to specific people in specific ways" |

Cross out anything that feels performed — what you think you *should* care about rather than what you actually do. What remains is closer to your actual values than any career planning exercise.

## The "Used to Love" Inventory

This exercise is for people whose sense of purpose was disrupted — by burnout, by prolonged stress, by a life event that folded everything in on itself. The standard "What do you love?" question fails here because the disruption has blocked access to what used to animate you.

The reframe: answer from *before* the disruption, not from now.

- What did I used to lose track of time doing?
- What did I used to find genuinely funny, before I got careful?
- What did I used to think about in the shower, before my thoughts became worry?
- What would a close friend from 5 years ago say I cared about?
- What would I be doing if I knew I'd be respected for it no matter what?

> The disruption has not erased your values. It has temporarily blocked access to them. The values are still there — the task is recovering the thread, not building from scratch.

The "Used to Love" inventory is more precise than the Energy Audit for people in this situation because it separates the pre-disruption self (who had clear values) from the post-disruption self (who has lost access to them). The recovery path is through the pre-disruption data.

## Values vs. Wants

One complication: most people conflate values with wants, and wants are often calibrated to what seems available rather than what is genuinely cared about.

The test: **would you care about this even if no one were watching?**

- Want: "I want to be a manager" — calibrated to what seems available in the organizational structure
- Value: "I want to develop the capacity to lead people well" — holds regardless of organizational context

When someone says "I don't know what I want," the more useful question is: what would you care about if you knew you'd be respected for it no matter what? That answer is closer to values than the first answer that comes to mind.

## The Paradoxical Note on Values Work

Values clarification can become another form of avoidance — spending months sorting cards, reading about values, talking about values, without acting on any of them.

The paradox: **values are not meant to be understood. They are meant to be enacted.**

Understanding values without acting on them produces the uncomfortable state of *knowing what matters but not living it*. This is worse than not knowing — it creates the sense of falling short of your own standard continuously.

The person who acts imperfectly in the direction of their values moves forward. The person who perfects their values list without acting is still back at the list.

The antidote: after any values clarification session, pick one small action that is a genuine expression of the value and do it within 24 hours. The action is the point, not the understanding.

## Values and the Other Frameworks

**Values and SDT:** Values clarification is the practical expression of the autonomy need. When you know what you value (independent of external approval), you have the internal compass that makes autonomous action possible. The person who has clarified their values is not driven by external regulation — they are moving in a self-determined direction.

**Values and Identity (Clear):** James Clear's identity work is about acting in accordance with who you want to become. Values are what give the "who you want to become" its content. Without values, identity is empty. Without identity, values are abstract. The combination — acting as the type of person who values X — is where sustainable purpose lives.

**Values and Ikigai:** The Japanese understanding of ikigai is essentially a values-based frame. Ikigai is not a grand achievement — it is the daily practice of moving in directions that matter, often small and private. The person who knows what they value and acts on it daily has an ikigai, regardless of whether they have a career or a calling in the Western sense.

**Values and Frankl:** Frankl's attitudinal values connect to this framework directly. Your values — what you care about, what direction you want to move in — cannot be taken from you by circumstances. You can lose your job, your health, your relationships. Your values remain intact. The attitudinal value is choosing your stance toward what remains — which requires knowing what you value in the first place.

## Quick Diagnostic Questions

Use these to determine whether a values clarification issue is present:

1. **"I don't know what I want"** → Values question, not wants question. What would you care about if no one were watching?

2. **"I knew what mattered before but everything fell apart"** → Used to Love inventory. The values are not gone — access is blocked.

3. **"I know what matters but I can't act on it"** → Usually a fusion problem (the thought of acting while overwhelmed creates collapse). Smallest possible action in the direction of the value, not a perfect action.

4. **"I achieved my goals but feel empty"** → Goals vs. values problem. Achieved the destination, lost the direction. Need to find the next valued direction to move in.

5. **"Everything feels meaningless"** → Sometimes the values themselves are unclear or abstract ("I want to help people" is too vague to act on). Narrowing to specific, concrete values usually resolves the paralysis.

## The Values-Action Gap

The most common failure in values work is the gap between knowing and acting. Two mechanisms typically cause it:

**Fusion:** The thought of acting ("I should be doing something that matters") creates enough anxiety to prevent action. This is not a motivation problem — it is a cognitive overload problem. The fix: reduce the scope of the action to something tiny enough that the anxiety doesn't block it. "Write one sentence about what I value" — not "clarify my entire life direction."

**Burnout:** When someone is already depleted, any action can feel like too much. The fix: identify the value that requires the least energy to express (often something small and private — taking a walk, reading for pleasure, playing music) and start there. The momentum of acting in accordance with values even in small ways reverses the depletion.

> The values-action gap is not evidence that your values are wrong. It is evidence that the gap between knowing and acting needs a bridge. The bridge is always smaller than you think.
