# Post-Traumatic Growth (PTG): Research Notes

*Source: Tedeschi & Calhoun, "Posttraumatic Growth: A New Frontier in Research" (1996) and subsequent literature.*

## Core Finding

30–70% of people who survive catastrophic events report positive psychological transformation afterward. PTG is not about being resilient in the moment — it is about the deep psychological reconstruction that can follow crisis.

## The Five Domains of PTG

1. **New strength** — discovering "I am stronger than I knew"
2. **New possibilities** — new horizons and options that weren't visible before
3. **Richer relationships** — some relationships deepen dramatically; others fall away
4. **Greater appreciation of life** — a shift in what registers as important
5. **Renewed purpose** — a deeper sense of calling or meaning that integrates the experience

## Growth and Grief Are Not Opposites

The toxic-positivity misreading: "After trauma, you either grieve or you grow." The research is clear: they coexist. Grief is not a failure to grow. Growth does not require eliminating grief. The person who is grieving AND growing is the norm, not the exception.

## Frankl's Anti-Nihilism Test Applied to Trauma

Frankl's framework asks: *Does this meaning pass the anti-nihilism test — does it hold up under scrutiny, connect you to something real, and not require denying what happened?*

Applied to PTG:
- **Does the growth narrative require denying the trauma?** → Invalid. PTG integrates the experience, not erasing it.
- **Does it connect you to something real?** → Valid growth is grounded in reality, not a positive spin.
- **Does it hold up over time?** → Sustainable growth does not collapse when the emotion fades.

## Growth Is Not Automatic

Tedeschi and Calhoun distinguish PTG from resilience:
- **Resilience** = returning to baseline after trauma
- **PTG** = genuine transformation beyond baseline, often deeper than pre-trauma functioning in some domains

Both can coexist. Neither negates the other.

## Conversation Scripts for When PTG Is Relevant

**When someone says:** *"After what happened, I can't imagine rebuilding."*

> The struggle to make sense of it is the engine of growth. The people who discover meaning late — after tragedy — are not special. They are people who stopped waiting for the experience to make sense on its own and started actively looking for what could be built from it. The question is not "why did this happen?" — it is "what do I do with what happened?"

**When someone says:** *"I feel guilty for saying anything positive about what I went through."*

> That guilt is the collision between grief and growth. Both are real. The growth does not mean you wanted the trauma. It means something grew inside the space the trauma created. Frankl would call this "meaning discovered in suffering" — not because suffering is good, but because human beings are capable of finding and building meaning even in circumstances they did not choose.

**When someone is stuck in fixed grieving and wants to explore growth:**

> "Some people find it useful to ask: five years from now, when you've rebuilt — what will you be able to do or be that you couldn't do before this? Not because the event was good. But because you had to build something new."
