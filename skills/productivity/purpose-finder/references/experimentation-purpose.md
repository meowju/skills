# Experimentation: The Mechanism of Purpose Discovery

This reference collects deeper material on the experimentation framework — research background, additional protocols, and edge cases not covered in the main SKILL.md section.

---

## Research Background

### Why Experiments Outperform Introspection

The evidence from career development research is consistent: people who find direction through structured experimentation reach clarity faster than those who rely on self-assessment instruments alone. The mechanism is straightforward — real behavior generates data that imagination cannot.

Dweck's **growth mindset** research shows that people who treat ability as developed (rather than fixed) are more willing to run experiments because failure is reframed as information rather than identity threat. This matters for purpose-searching: if failure at a 30-day experiment feels like "I am the kind of person who fails," you won't run enough experiments to find direction. The reframe — "this specific thing didn't work, I have new data" — is the prerequisite for experimental purpose-searching.

Kangan's work on **career adaptability** identifies four dimensions: concern (future orientation), control ( decisional autonomy), curiosity (exploration), and confidence (self-efficacy). Purpose experiments directly exercise all four. The person who runs 12 experiments in a year is practicing career adaptability — which is both the means and the outcome.

### The "Minimum Viable Direction" Concept

From lean startup methodology applied to life design: a **minimum viable direction** is the smallest commitment you can make that generates useful information about whether a direction is worth pursuing. It has three components:

1. **Hypothesis:** "I think I might care about X"
2. **Experiment:** A specific action taken to test the hypothesis
3. **Signal:** What success looks like — what would have to be true in the world for you to know the hypothesis is promising?

The signal must be observable and specific. "I'll know it when I feel it" is not a signal. "I want to keep doing this after the initial novelty wears off" is closer.

### Effort vs. Outcome Distinction

A critical distinction in experiment design: **you can control effort, you cannot control outcome.** When you design a purpose experiment, you commit to showing up and doing the work — not to producing a particular result.

This matters because most people who "try something and it doesn't work" tried to control the outcome and then quit when the outcome didn't match their expectation. The experiment is not "did I become good at this in 30 days?" The experiment is "did I engage genuinely for 30 days, and what did I learn about my engagement from that engagement?"

## The Experiment Protocol: Detailed Version

### Before the Experiment

**Hypothesis formulation (10 minutes):**
Write one sentence: "I think I might care about [X] because [observable evidence that brought me here]."

The "because" is important — it names the signal that led you to this hypothesis. If you can't articulate why you think you might care about this, the hypothesis is too vague to test.

**Commitment window:**
Set a specific end date. Write it on the calendar. Experiments without end dates become indefinite and lose their information value.

**Minimum engagement bar:**
Define what "real effort" looks like for this specific experiment. For a writing experiment: "I will write and publish at least 8 pieces over 4 weeks." For a business experiment: "I will have 10 customer conversations over 3 weeks." The bar must be high enough to generate real data but low enough that you can actually hit it if you try.

### During the Experiment

**Weekly pulse (5 minutes at end of each week):**

1. On a scale of 1–5, how engaged was I this week? (1 = I had to force myself, 5 = I lost track of time multiple times)
2. What surprised me this week?
3. What do I want to try differently next week?

**Energy tracking:**
At the end of each day during the experiment, note: (a) did I want to keep going on this when the session ended? (b) did I think about it when I wasn't doing it? These two signals are the early indicators of genuine engagement.

**The "would I do this if no one was watching?" test:**
During the experiment, occasionally ask: if no one would ever know whether I did this, would I still do it? Intrinsic motivation — not driven by external validation — is the more reliable signal of genuine interest.

### After the Experiment

**The three-signal check-in (use at 30, 60, 90 days):**

| Signal | Question | Evidence to look for |
|--------|----------|---------------------|
| Energy | Did I want to continue this? | Did I keep voluntarily returning to this after initial novelty? |
| Growth | Am I getting better? | Can I do things now that I couldn't do at the start? |
| World | Does this matter to anyone else? | Do people respond to what I'm doing? Is there any external validation of the work? |

**The hard question:**
If the data says "no" on all three — or even on two of three — the experiment produced useful information: this direction, in this form, is not where your energy wants to go. The failure is real, and the information is valuable. The only mistake is continuing to invest time in a direction the data has already contradicted.

### When to Extend vs. Pivot

After a 30-day experiment, the decision is binary: extend or pivot. Use these heuristics:

- **Extend** if: engagement is growing (not just stable), you're getting better, and something in the world is responding
- **Pivot** if: you had to force yourself every time, you're not getting measurably better, and no one responds to what you're doing

After a 90-day immersive test, the bar for extending is higher. You have now given this genuine effort. If the signals are still ambiguous or negative at 90 days with real effort invested, the information is strong. Extend only if you genuinely want to keep going — not out of obligation or sunk cost.

## The Most Common Experiment Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Half-effort and calling it a verdict

The person tries something for 3 days, finds it hard, and declares "I tried it and it's not for me." This is the most common and most destructive mistake in purpose-searching. Getting not-terrible at something requires approximately 20 hours of deliberate practice. At hour 3, you are not good at it — by design. You are supposed to be bad at it. The "I tried and it wasn't for me" verdict requires at least 20 hours of genuine effort before it is valid.

### Mistake 2: Treating outcome as the measure

"I wrote a piece and it got no reads, so writing isn't for me." The experiment was about engagement, not external validation. A piece getting no reads is data about the specific piece, not about whether writing is your direction. The signal for writing is: did you want to keep writing? Did you learn something from writing it? Did the process of creating it produce something you cared about? External response is world-signal and matters at stage 3, but at stage 2 it is not the primary measure.

### Mistake 3: Only testing the familiar

People tend to experiment with things that feel safe or known. If you only experiment within your current comfort zone, you are not actually exploring — you are confirming. The purpose experiment should sometimes feel a little outside your current ability. If the experiment is easy, you're not stretching far enough to generate new information.

### Mistake 4: Running experiments alone

Experiments are more informative when you do them in public — even minimally. Publishing a piece, showing your prototype to one person, asking for feedback on your work. Solitary experiments generate internal data (how did I feel?) which is valuable but incomplete. External feedback helps calibrate: does this thing you are building connect to anything real in other people's experience?

### Mistake 5: Not keeping records

Without a log, you lose the data. After 90 days of an immersive test, your memory of what you felt and thought is unreliable — it will be filtered through what you believe now about the direction. The weekly pulse notes are the data set you evaluate at the end, not your recollection of how it felt.

## Low-Effort Experiment Ideas (Level 1 Starters)

If you genuinely don't know what to try, start here — these cost almost nothing and generate some initial signal:

- **Observation journal (2 weeks):** At the end of each day, write one sentence: what absorbed me today? What did I think about when I had free attention? Don't filter for importance — just record.
- **Autobiography reading (2 weeks):** Read the life story of someone who did something you think you might want. Not a business book — a biography or memoir. Notice what you respond to and what you don't.
- **Shadow for a morning:** Find one person who does something you think you might want to do and spend 3 hours watching them do it. Not a career interview — just observation.
- **One-hour prototype:** Pick something that feels adjacent to an interest and spend one hour making a thing. Write something, draw something, code something, build something. The thing doesn't matter — the engagement does.

## Sequencing: What to Try First

When you have no clear hypothesis about direction, the prioritization for experiments:

1. **Try what you already think you might like** — you already have some signal, however faint
2. **Try what other people value you for** — your existing strengths are the lowest-friction entry point
3. **Try what scares you a little** — if it scares you, it might be new information
4. **Try what seems useful to other people** — contribution often precedes clarity

The order is not a rule — it's a heuristic for people who genuinely don't know where to start.

## Edge Cases

### Multiple Experiments Simultaneously

You can run multiple Level 1 and Level 2 experiments at once — that's fine, they're low-commitment. Do not run more than one Level 3 immersive test at the same time. Immersive tests require real effort, and real effort divided across multiple directions produces mediocre data across all of them instead of clear signal about one.

### Experimenting When Depressed

Depression distorts engagement signals — nothing feels good when you're depressed, which makes it impossible to use "did I enjoy this?" as a reliable measure. If someone is in clinical depression, the experimentation framework should be introduced after some mood improvement — not as the primary intervention. Purpose-searching while severely depressed usually produces unreliable data that then reinforces the depression ("I tried things and nothing worked, therefore I'm broken"). The first step is professional support, not experiments.

### When All Experiments Fail

This is worth addressing directly because it happens: the person runs multiple experiments and none produce positive signals. Before concluding "nothing works for me," check:

1. **Were the experiments run with genuine effort?** If you quit everything at hour 3, you haven't tested them.
2. **Were the experiments designed correctly?** Did you control for effort rather than outcome? Did you measure engagement rather than results?
3. **Was the form right, not the direction?** "Fiction didn't work" might mean "fiction in this specific form at this moment" rather than "writing isn't for me." Try a different form.
4. **Is this depression distoring the data?** If nothing engages at all — not just experiments but nothing in life — the question is about mood, not about purpose.

The person who genuinely cannot find any direction through experimentation is rare. Most people who believe they have this problem have actually been running insufficient experiments or evaluating them incorrectly.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **Career Capital (Newport):** Experiments are how you identify which career capital tracks are worth investing in. You experiment across domains, find where engagement is high and growth is possible, then go deep.
- **Flow (Csikszentmihalyi):** The energy signal in the 90-day check-in is flow data: did you lose track of time? Did the challenge pull you in? Experiments are the mechanism for finding activities that produce flow.
- **Identity Habits (Clear):** Each experiment is a vote for a type of person. Running experiments is itself an identity — the person who engages, who tries things, who collects data. That identity precedes and enables specific direction.
- **Founder Mode (Chesky):** Founders find direction through iteration — the same process as the experimentation framework. Build, observe what resonates, adjust. The founder's approach to building is an experimenter's approach to life.
